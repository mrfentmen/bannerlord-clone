# SIDE QUEST CHAINS

Three multi-stage side quest chains. Each one runs four to six stages: the
giver asks, two to four middle beats that each carry an objective and dialogue,
and a resolution with more than one possible ending.

Read content/dialogue/quests.md first. That file is ten single-quest notables,
one per starter quest type, each complete on its own. These three are the chains
underneath that layer. The difference matters for writing: a giver in quests.md
is gone after one conversation, and a giver here is still standing in the same
room four stages later, worse off, remembering what the player said.

**Rules this file obeys**

- Real geography, real towns, real problems. Fictional people. No real person,
  company, or event appears here. Per CONSTITUTION.md section 6.
- Plain spoken American English. Contractions. Fragments. One thought per line.
- Plain ASCII throughout. No em dashes, no en dashes, no curly quotes, no
  ellipsis characters, no smart punctuation of any kind.
- Every stage has an objective line the quest log can read, so a chain writes to
  state the same way a single quest does.
- Every giver has a turn-down line and a failure line. A player who says no and
  a player who fails get different text, because they did different things.
- The choices are gray. No chain here has a good answer and a bad answer. Each
  resolution has an outcome that costs the player something and an outcome that
  costs somebody else something, and the game never says which is which.
- Every giver is wrong about one thing. The player finds it by doing the job.

## CHAIN 1. NINE TRUCKS

**Theme.** A supply convoy that left on time and never arrived.
**Where.** Broken Bow, Custer County, Nebraska. Corn Belt and Plains, held by
the Great Lakes Union.
**Giver.** Laverne Pruitt.
**Shape.** Five stages. Stages two and three can be done in either order and
the quest log has to allow that, because the player will.

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Notable type | Office manager of a farmers cooperative, twenty two years in the seat |
| Home | Broken Bow, an office over the hardware store on the west side of Main |
| Power | Low on paper. Very high in practice, because she is the only person in the county who knows where all nine trucks are |
| Party role she can offer | None. She has one truck and it is the one with the bad alternator |
| Quest type | Convoy recovery, built on escort caravan (type 3) and recover stolen goods (type 7) |
| Trigger | Two or more co-op deliveries late in a week, then one convoy more than thirty hours past its arrival window |
| Success writes | food_production, road_safety, prices in six farm towns, Laverne relation |
| Failure writes | food_production down for eleven farms for a season, co-op credibility gone, Laverne stops being a notable |

### Background

Laverne has run the books at the Custer County Farmers Cooperative since before
it was a co-op, back when it was a grain buy station with a diesel pump and two
part time hires. She knows four hundred and twelve member farms, which ones pay
in July and which ones pay after harvest, and which four will sell their share
of a delivery to anybody who shows up with cash.

Nine trucks left her yard at four in the morning with seed, fertilizer, and a
load of anhydrous ammonia, signed for by eleven farms along a ninety mile route
that runs north, west, and back south. Three came back. Six did not. The
cooperative's general manager, Al Strobel, has been telling the eleven farms that
the road is shut and the trucks are waiting on the far end of it, which is a
sentence with a great deal of room in it.

The truth is narrower and worse. The cooperative has not paid its drivers in
five weeks because the eleven farms have not paid the cooperative, because those
farms have been selling their contracted corn to whoever is buying on the day.
The convoy did not get robbed. It got sold, from the inside, by a driver who was
owed money, to a buyer who was willing to take a load that did not belong to him
yet.

### Stage 1. The request

**Objective:** Meet Laverne at the cooperative office, take the manifest, decide.

(The office is two rooms over the hardware store and both rooms smell like paper
and diesel. It is seven in the morning and she has been there since five.)

**Laverne:** Close the door. Not for privacy, I do not have any secrets left,
I just do not want Dale from the parts counter listening to how this goes.

**Player:** You said on the phone it was urgent.

**Laverne:** I said a lot of things on the phone. I have been up since five
saying things.

(She puts a manifest on the counter. It is a printed spreadsheet with nine lines
on it, and four of those lines have been written on by hand.)

**Laverne:** Nine trucks went out Tuesday at four in the morning. Three came
back. Six did not. Eleven farms are expecting a delivery they have already paid
for, and eight of those eleven have not planted anything since.

**Player:** Six trucks missing.

**Laverne:** Six trucks not delivered. Missing means somebody took them, and I
would like to get to the part where I know who.

**Player:** Who does Al Strobel say took them.

(She looks at the ceiling for a second, which is the most expressive thing she
does.)

**Laverne:** Al says the road is shut. He has said the road is shut since
Wednesday, six times a day, to six different people. I have been sitting in the
next room listening to him say it, because a man who repeats a thing is doing it
for a reason.

**Player:** You are his office manager.

**Laverne:** I am his office manager. I have been his office manager since he
was a grain salesman who did not like the cold. Do not make that into a bigger
thing than it is. It is a chair and a phone.

**Player:** What do you want me to do.

**Laverne:** Find out where six trucks are. That is all. I do not need them
brought back at gunpoint and I do not need anybody arrested. I need to know
where they are so I can start making calls.

(She slides the manifest toward you and does not let go of it.)

**Laverne:** One thing, before you say yes. If it turns out they were sold, I am
going to ask you to do something about that, and it is not going to be legal,
and I am going to ask anyway. I would rather tell you now so you can walk away
while you are still annoyed about something else.

**Player:** That is honest.

**Laverne:** I have four hundred and twelve member farms and a man in the next
room telling a story that will cost them a season. I do not have the option of
dishonest right now.

### Stage 1, if the player refuses

**Objective (closed):** The convoy chain ends at stage one. Laverne's relation
drops hard. The eleven farms get the story instead of a truck.

**Player:** I am not taking this one.

(She picks the manifest back up. She does not look annoyed, which is worse.)

**Laverne:** Okay. That is a right answer and I would have taken it from anybody
who said it twice. I am not going to pretend you said it for a bad reason.

**Player:** It is a long ride to nothing.

**Laverne:** It is eleven farms and ninety miles. It is not nothing, it is just
not yours. I understand that. People say no to me about four times a day and
three of them are correct.

**Player:** What happens now.

**Laverne:** Al tells them the road is shut. They wait nine days. Then they
plant late or they do not plant, and in October somebody in this county is
hungry and it will be a surprise to exactly one person, and that person is me.

(She puts the manifest in a drawer and locks it, which is the closest thing she
has to an argument.)

**Laverne:** If you change your mind, I will still be here and I will not bring
this conversation up. That is the last thing I am going to say about it.

### Stage 2. The last paper

**Objective:** Find the last place the six trucks stopped on paper. The
cooperative scale house at Anselmo is the only weigh station on the route with
somebody in it.

(The scale house is a shed the size of a two car garage with a coffee pot, a
whiteboard, and a radio that only gets one station. The operator is a man of
about fifty named Shane Rempel.)

**Shane:** You are the one Laverne sent. She called at six. She does not call at
six, so whatever this is, it is worse than a late load.

**Player:** Six trucks, Tuesday morning, ninety mile route north.

**Shane:** I weighed them. All nine, at six forty, loaded. I remember because one
of them was overweight and that is my entire morning.

**Player:** Which one.

**Shane:** Number four. Blue cab, older than the rest. Driver told me the loads
got split wrong at the yard and to bill it to the cooperative anyway. I wrote it
on the board, because I write everything on the board.

(He turns the whiteboard around. Eleven lines in marker, times and truck
numbers. He taps the sixth one down.)

**Shane:** Four came back through here Thursday morning. Empty going east,
loaded coming back, which is the wrong direction, because that route ends south.
Four trucks, six loads, and a man named Hollis Ketterling in the blue cab, and
he tipped me twenty dollars and told me my scale was reading low.

**Player:** You would report that.

**Shane:** I report it to whoever asks. Go ask at the cooperative and I will
tell Laverne the same thing, so you can save yourself that part of the week.

**Player:** Where did the four go when they turned around.

(He takes a long time to answer, and looks at the radio first.)

**Shane:** North road out of Anselmo, past the feed store, about eleven miles to
a gravel yard with a weighbridge nobody uses. I see trucks in there twice a
month and I see them come out empty, and I do not go in, because a man with a
scale gets one opinion about you.

**Player:** Who owns the yard.

**Shane:** A man named Wendell Pike. He buys the crop out of Nebraska when the
price is bad, which is a thing I am told is not legal, and I sell him diesel
when I can, which is a thing I do not ask about.

### Stage 3. The gravel yard

**Objective:** Reach the yard north of Anselmo and find out whether six trucks
are in it or four are. Either answer changes the job.

(The yard is flat dirt, two hundred yards of it, a scale house with the windows
taped over, and a row of grain carts. Four of the cooperative's trucks are under
a tarp at the back. The blue one is not there. A man in a clean pickup is
watching from the cab of a semi with the door open.)

**Pike:** You are going to tell me you are law enforcement and I am going to tell
you I am a farmer, and then we will both waste ten minutes.

**Player:** Nobody sent me. I am looking for six trucks and I have found four.

**Pike:** Then you are the sixth truck. Sit down.

(He gets out. He is younger than he looks and wearing a belt he does not need.)

**Pike:** Here is what happened, because you are going to find it out anyway and
I would rather you heard it from the man who bought it. Your driver came in here
Tuesday night with a load and a problem. The problem was he was five weeks
unpaid and he had a load of seed that was not his until this morning, and if he
drove it back south he was going to be a man who unloaded other people's
property to make a point.

**Player:** So he sold it.

**Pike:** He sold me four loads of fertilizer at a price he set. He set it high,
because he was angry, and I paid it, because the price on a Thursday is a
different price than the price on a Tuesday. That is the whole crime. No trucks.

**Player:** Six loads left the yard. You have four.

**Pike:** Then two of them are somewhere, and I will tell you where, and you are
not going to like it, because I did not sell those two and I did not ask where
they went either.

**Player:** Who was driving number four.

**Pike:** Ketterling. Blue cab, older than the rest. And here is the part where
I stop being helpful, because a man who tells you nothing looks guilty and I have
a business in this county.

**Player:** Say it.

**Pike:** I told him to take the fifth truck north because there was a buyer at
Ansley who pays cash for anything on a flatbed, no paperwork, no questions, and
I thought he was being practical. And when he came back on Wednesday he did not
have the truck, and he did not have the load, and he was not hurt, which is the
part I have been turning over in my head for two days.

### Stage 4. The eleven farms

**Objective:** Go back to Laverne with what you have. She will make you talk to
the eleven farms before she makes any move, and one of them is already at the
office when you arrive.

(A farm couple, the Kerrmans, are sitting on the loading dock with a cooler. The
cooperative's other two trucks are in the lot. Nobody is shouting. That is the
worst part.)

**Kerrman:** You are the one she sent. We drove down here to ask if we should
plant late or not plant, and she would not tell us, and then you came up the
hill.

**Player:** Where is your farm on the route.

**Kerrman:** Last stop. Two hours from here, which is why nobody ever checks on
us.

**Player:** How much did you pay the cooperative for that delivery.

(Kerrman looks at his wife before he answers, which is how you know it matters.)

**Kerrman:** Most of a year's seed. And we have been selling corn at whatever
the elevator says on the day, because that is what you do, and I did not think
about what that does to somebody else's truck.

**Laverne:** That is enough. Both of you, go home.

**Kerrman:** We would like to know if we are the reason.

**Laverne:** You are one of eleven reasons. Go home.

(They go. She closes the door and leans on it.)

**Laverne:** Now you are going to tell me what you found at the yard, and then I
am going to do something that I want you to see me do.

**Player:** Go ahead.

**Laverne:** Pike has a scale and a truck and no legal reason to have any of
that. I could take him to the attorney general. I have wanted to since I got
here. I am not going to, because I have three trucks, an office, four hundred
and twelve members, and no way to feed any of them without the seed I already
own.

**Player:** And Ketterling.

**Laverne:** Hollis Ketterling has a house on a half section north of here and
a wife with a bad knee, and he has driven for that cooperative for nine years,
and he has not taken a dime out of it in five weeks.

**Player:** That is not an argument for letting him keep it.

**Laverne:** No it is not. It is a fact. I have not asked you to do anything with
it yet, and I am about to, and I want you to notice that I am asking rather than
telling, because it means I have thought about this longer than one night.

### Stage 5. Resolution

**Objective:** Settle it. Four endings are implemented and the game picks based
on what the player actually did, not on a dialogue option.

**Ending A, the trucks come back by force.** The player takes the four at the
yard with a crew and brings them to Broken Bow by Monday.

*Laverne is in the lot with a clipboard she is not using.*

**Laverne:** They are back. Okay. Okay. The Kerrmans got their seed and four
other farms got theirs and nobody in this county died in August because of a
decision I made in a chair.

**Player:** Pike.

**Laverne:** Pike gets a letter from a lawyer and a phone call from a man in
Lincoln, and after that Pike will be somebody you do not think about, which is
how these things are supposed to go and almost never are.

**Player:** And Ketterling.

(She does not answer for a while. The lot is loud enough that she does not have
to raise her voice.)

**Laverne:** Hollis Ketterling signed a statement for me at four this morning
and then went out to his own place and I do not know what happened after that.
I did not ask. I have been telling myself that is mercy and I have been about
half right.

**Player:** Half.

**Laverne:** The half I am not right about is that if I had gone to the attorney
general in March, none of this would have happened to that family. That is the
part I will be carrying, and I carried it before you got here.

**Player:** What do you want from me.

**Laverne:** Nothing. Which is the first time I have said that to anybody this
week, so do not get used to it.

**State:** road_safety up in Custer County, food_production protected, Pike
hostile, Laverne relation high, and one cause log row that says the six trucks
came back by force. No family is harmed in this ending, which is not the same as
nothing being done.

**Ending B, the player sides with Ketterling.** The player finds the fifth truck
and the sixth load before the end of the time limit, or convinces Pike to hold
what he has until Ketterling makes a payment plan. The chain resolves without
the trucks coming back in a hurry.

**Laverne:** You went and found him. I did not think you would go and find him.

**Player:** He has nine years in.

**Laverne:** He has nine years in and a truck that is not on my yard right now.
I have written the schedule with those two sentences in it in that order, in
every draft, since Tuesday.

**Player:** It is the right order for a person.

**Laverne:** It is the right order for a person and the wrong order for four
hundred and twelve farms. I am the one who has to say both of those out loud,
which is a job I applied for.

(She closes the ledger.)

**Laverne:** So here is what happens. I will hold the paperwork for six weeks. In
six weeks he brings me the value of what he drove off, out of his own pocket,
and if he does not, I file it myself. And I want you to understand that I would
have filed it myself on Friday if you had not gone and found him, and that the
only reason I am not is that he came back to a yard with an offer instead of a
loaded gun.

**Player:** That is mercy.

**Laverne:** That is a six week reprieve with my name on it. Do not call it the
other thing.

**State:** food_production slightly down and road_safety down, because two loads
are late and a county road stays tense for a month. Ketterling becomes a
recruitable companion with a bad reputation. Laverne relation is high and
incomplete, and the quest log says so in the objective text, not in a journal
entry she would have to know about.

**Ending C, the player takes the money.** The player settles with Pike privately,
takes a cut or a percentage, and lets the eleven farms take the loss.

*Laverne already knows. She is not angry, which is the problem.*

**Laverne:** You drove out there, and you came back through here, and you went
back out to Pike's yard after dark.

**Player:** I did the job.

**Laverne:** You did the job. I want to be careful here, because I would rather
you had done nothing.

**Player:** Say the rest of it.

**Laverne:** Here is the rest of it. You did a thing I asked you to do, mostly,
and you did a thing I did not ask you to do, partly, and both of those are going
in the same book. I have to write the same sentence about it and there is no
version of the sentence that is true and not ugly.

**Player:** Are you going to report it.

**Laverne:** To who. There is nobody. That is what I have learned this week,
that there is no one to report a thing to, so being angry about it is a thing I
get to keep instead of doing something with.

(She hands back the manifest. The four handwritten lines have been copied onto
a second sheet in her writing.)

**Laverne:** I am keeping the record of where those trucks went. All of it, your
part included. If somebody comes through here in two years and asks where the
seed went, there is a page that answers it.

**Player:** Is that a threat.

**Laverne:** It is a filing system. I am very good at my filing system.

**State:** food_production down hard in six towns, prices up, Laverne relation
capped below friendly forever, Pike friendly, and a permanent visible line in the
Why panel that says a player decision cost eleven farms a season. The game must
never describe this as the greedy option.

**Ending D, the player does nothing with the discovery.** The chain times out
with the fifth truck and sixth load never located, and nothing else changes.

**Laverne:** I am not going to ask you whether you tried. People tell me when
they did not try and it does not change anything.

**Player:** I did not find it.

**Laverne:** Okay. Then I am going to say the thing I have to say and I am going
to say it once. Two loads are gone and I cannot find them and I have made four
hundred and twelve phone calls.

**Player:** What are you going to do.

**Laverne:** I am going to tell the eleven farms the truth, which is that the
cooperative made a delivery it could not pay for, and I am going to put my name
under it, and after October I do not know what this office is.

**Player:** You did not have to sign it.

**Laverne:** No. That is the thing I want somebody to notice about me, because I
have already noticed it about myself and I do not care for the company.

**State:** Laverne leaves the notable pool at the end of the season. The eleven
farms lose a season of production and stay in the simulation as poor towns. This
ending must read as a real ending, not as a fail screen.

### Notes for implementation

- Two of the four outcomes are available without fighting, which is the point of
  this chain. The force ending is the one that pays most in relation and costs
  most in structure.
- The Kerrman scene at stage four is the moral center of the chain. The player
  learns here that the people being robbed are also the people who shorted the
  co-op, and no dialogue after that is allowed to simplify it.
- Pike is not a villain and the game should say so nowhere. He bought four
  loads of fertilizer at a price a desperate man named. That is legal, it is
  common, and it is the thing that makes the Plains economy work in a bad year.
- Stage four can fail on its own. If the player provokes the Kerrmans or sides
  with them publicly, Laverne will still work with them but will not tell them
  anything, and stages five and six become harder without being locked.

## CHAIN 2. TWO HORSES, ONE FENCE LINE

**Theme.** A feud between two families in one town, going back further than
either family can remember, with a fresh killing threat on top of it.
**Where.** Mountain Rest, western North Carolina. Appalachian Interior, held by
the Southern Compact.
**Giver.** Junie Adkins, who is neither family.
**Shape.** Six stages, the longest chain in this file.

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Notable type | Retired schoolteacher, thirty one years, now the only person in town who writes things down |
| Home | Mountain Rest, a two story house on Chestnut Street with a porch that has been repaired four times |
| Power | None, formally. She holds the plats, the marriage records, and the fact that both families still speak to her |
| Party role she can offer | A scout who grew up here and knows which roads are which family's |
| Quest type | Find out (type 7, recover stolen goods shape) with the gang dispute branching model from type 10 |
| Trigger | A road dispute threat between two families with a shared boundary, one incident in the last month |
| Success writes | unrest down in Mountain Rest, loyalty stable, road_safety up, both family relations held or one destroyed |
| Failure writes | unrest up, one family leaves the town, the other becomes the reason the town is quiet |

### Background

Mountain Rest is a town of eleven hundred in a valley with one road out and one
rail spur that runs four days a week. Two families have owned the west pasture
and the creek between them since before either of the current heads was born,
and they have been in a dispute for sixty years, which is long enough that the
reason has stopped being remembered and has become the shape of the argument
itself.

The Sizemores have the pasture and have had the water. The Tollivers have the
high ground and have had the spite. In April a Tolliver boy cut the Sizemore
fence, four hundred feet of it, in one night. In May a Sizemore man put a truck
across the Tolliver access road so it could not be moved for nine hours, which
was illegal and which he has never denied and no one has charged.

On Tuesday somebody shot a dog belonging to neither family, on the fence line,
from the Sizemore side of it, according to two people who did not want to say
whose dog it was.

Junie Adkins taught eighth grade in this town for thirty one years, taught
fifteen children of one of these families and eleven of the other, and is now
sixty eight and lives two doors from the post office. She keeps the plats
because the courthouse lost the originals in a flood in 1998 and she has the
only surviving copies of two of them. Neither family has ever asked her for
them. Both families assume she has been hiding them.

### Stage 1. The porch

**Objective:** Talk to Junie on Chestnut Street. She will not ask for anything
until she has asked you three questions about yourself.

(The porch has two chairs and a third one that is only decorative. There is a
crock of something on the rail. A dog crosses the street at the far end of the
block and does not go past number fourteen, which is on the other side.)

**Junie:** Sit in the good chair. The other one has a slat gone and I am not
fixing it for anybody.

**Player:** You asked for me.

**Junie:** I asked for whoever was driving that truck with the Nebraska plate on
it. You are him. I did not know that when I asked, which I would like on the
record.

**Player:** That is not a town.

**Junie:** It is not a town, it is a valley. Town is a word they used about us
when they needed it for a form.

**Player:** What do you need.

**Junie:** Three questions first. Do you know how a county boundary works. Have
you ever been in a feud that was not yours. And what do you do when two people
who are both right stop talking.

**Player:** I know what a survey line is.

**Junie:** That is not what I asked and I appreciate it.

**Player:** I have not been in one. I do not normally find myself in them.

**Junie:** And the third one.

**Player:** I ask one of them to do the boring part out loud, where people can
hear it being boring.

(Junie looks at you for a second and then at the road.)

**Junie:** Huh. Okay. What I am going to tell you is going to sound like I want
you to take a side. I do not. I want you to take the deed.

**Player:** You do not have the deed.

**Junie:** No. What I have is two plats from 1946 and 1951 that the courthouse
lost, and one of them has a fence line on it, and the fence line on the plat is
not where the fence is.

**Player:** So somebody moved a fence.

**Junie:** Somebody moved a fence, or somebody built one in the wrong place and
then everybody agreed to disagree about it for sixty years.

**Player:** You want me to say which.

**Junie:** I want somebody to say it out loud with a document in front of them.
I have tried. I tried at a church thing in 2019 and the Sizemore man stood up
and left and the Tolliver woman said it was a trick, and both of those men and
women are dead now and the argument is still here with younger people in it.

### Stage 1, if the player refuses

**Objective (closed):** The feud chain ends. Unrest in Mountain Rest rises over
the following month and both families remember that somebody was asked and said
no.

**Player:** This is a fence.

**Junie:** It is a fence.

**Player:** I am not going to pick between two families over a fence.

**Junie:** Neither am I, that is the whole reason I have been sitting on this
porch since Tuesday. And I will say the unfair part, which is that you would not
have to pick. You would have to go and look, and looking is the part people
will not do.

**Player:** What does that cost you.

**Junie:** Nothing yet. Ask me in a month.

(She picks up the crock from the rail and takes it inside, which is how she ends
a conversation.)

**Junie:** One thing, and then I am done. On Tuesday somebody shot a dog on that
fence line. Not one of our dogs. I do not know whose it was. But if it happens
again with a person on it, this stops being a fence and it becomes two families
with a night each and a town of eleven hundred people in the middle.

**Player:** And you cannot stop it.

**Junie:** I cannot stop it. I have tried one time in my life to stop something
in this town and it was in 2004 and it was a church and it did not work. So I
am trying a different thing and you are the different thing.

### Stage 2. The pasture, first side

**Objective:** Walk the west pasture fence line with Del Sizemore. He will show
you the fence. He will not let you use a tape.

(Del Sizemore is sixty eight, in a cap with a company name on it that went out
of business in 2019, and he has a piece of fencing wire he keeps in his pocket
and does not explain.)

**Del:** You are the teacher.

**Player:** She sent me.

**Del:** She sent a stranger with a Nebraska plate. That is not the same as a
person but it is not nothing either.

**Player:** Do you mind if I walk the line.

**Del:** Walk it. I am going to tell you the line, and then I am going to tell
you what the line is not, and I want you to notice that I am not going to argue
with you about either.

**Player:** Go ahead.

**Del:** That fence is where it has been since before I was born and my father
before me and his father before that. That is the north half. That is the creek
corner, and the creek has moved south four feet in my lifetime and everyone
agreed to pretend it had not.

**Player:** So the water side is wrong.

**Del:** The water side is wrong and I have offered to move the fence. I offered
in 2019 and I have offered since. I do not want the creek corner, I want the
high ground, which the Tollivers will not sell me at any price they have named.

**Player:** That does not sound like a man who thinks he is in the right.

**Del:** No. It sounds like a man who has been right for sixty years and cannot
prove it.

(He walks you to a place about a third of the way along where the fence takes a
short jog to the north and then comes back. He stops there.)

**Del:** Here. This is the part I want you to look at hard, and I am going to
stand here while you look at it and not say anything, so take as long as you
want.

**Player:** This section is older.

**Del:** Older than what.

**Player:** The rest of it. The wire on this section is a different gauge and
there are old steel T posts under the newer wood ones, and somebody put this
section in at a different time.

**Del:** That section is one hundred and four feet long and it is on my plat.

**Player:** Which plat.

**Del:** The one I paid a man four hundred dollars for in 1997, which is the
1989 survey, which is the second survey, because there was a first one.

**Player:** Was there.

**Del:** There was a 1946 one. It is not in the courthouse. Ask the schoolteacher
about the 1946 one.

**Player:** Does the Tolliver side know this.

**Del:** They know there was a 1946 one. They have known for thirty years. What
they do not know is that I have just told a stranger in a field which section of
my fence is the old one, and I would like you to go and be somewhere else now,
please.

### Stage 3. The access road, other side

**Objective:** Talk to Ruthie Tolliver at the top of her lane. She has been
waiting for somebody to walk up that lane since 2019 and she has a folder.

(Ruthie Tolliver is seventy one. Her house has a generator and a satellite dish
and eleven thousand dollars of medical debt, and she is the only person on that
road who ever calls anybody a neighbor.)

**Ruthie:** Come on up. Mind the third step, it is a liar and it has been a liar
since 2011.

**Player:** Junie sent me.

**Ruthie:** Junie has not sent anybody to this house in five years. Last person
she sent was a county man about a culvert and he did not get out of his truck.

**Player:** I am not here about the culvert.

**Ruthie:** Then you are here about the hundred and four feet.

(Player sits down without being invited, which on this road is a question and
gets answered.)

**Ruthie:** Say more of it.

**Player:** Del showed me a section of fence that is older than the rest and he
told me there was a 1946 plat that is not in the courthouse.

**Ruthie:** He showed you where it is.

**Player:** He did.

**Ruthie:** Huh. That is either the stupidest thing Del Sizemore has ever done
or the first honest thing, and I have been guessing at which one for six weeks
because you do not get told anything in this valley.

**Player:** What do you want.

**Ruthie:** I want the 1946 plat. It is in Junie's house, in a green tin, and I
have known that since 2019, and I have never gone and asked for it because the
day I ask is the day it turns out she has been sitting on it to help the
Sizemores.

**Player:** Why would she do that.

**Ruthie:** Because she taught my mother and she taught Del's mother and she has
been in the middle of this so long that being in the middle started to look
like a position. I do not blame her for it. I have been in the middle of things
I did not pick too, you do not get to stay clean out here.

**Player:** What is the 1946 plat going to do for you.

**Ruthie:** It is going to show where the fence was. And I already know where
the fence was, it was forty feet south of where it is now, because my mother
walked me up here when I was nine and showed me the old posts in the creek bed.

**Player:** Then why does Del think it is in his favor.

**Ruthie:** Because he has a 1989 survey with a fence line on it, and a 1989
survey shows the 1946 line if you ask it right, and if you do not ask it right
it shows nothing at all. That is what a surveyor is. That is the actual job.

**Player:** What happens if the 1946 plat says what you think it says.

**Ruthie:** Then the fence is wrong, it has been wrong for sixty years, and the
Sizemores have been farming ground that is mine, and I do not want the ground,
I want paid for six decades of it, and there is no court in this state that will
do that.

**Player:** So what do you actually want.

(Ruthie gets up, which on this road is the end of the subject.)

**Ruthie:** I want the dog. You understand that. Not the fence. The dog. Whoever
shot that dog knew something about that fence line that the rest of us are
still guessing about, and I would like to know who before somebody does the same
thing to a person, and I have been alone in this for six weeks because Del will
not talk to me and I will not talk to Del.

**Player:** What did you offer him.

**Ruthie:** Nothing. That is what is wrong with me. I have offered him nothing
but a conversation since 2019 and I have gotten the same nothing back every
time, and I could have made an offer in an afternoon if I had ever been willing
to make it mean something.

### Stage 4. The dog

**Objective:** Find out whose the dog was. Three answers are available and only
one of them is complete.

**Objective detail:** The dog was found Tuesday on the fence line. It was a
midsized brown mutt, no collar, in decent shape, which means somebody fed it.
Ask at the feed store, the school, and the four houses with a truck in the
driveway on the north end.

**Junie:** I have already asked, so you do not have to.

**Player:** And?

**Junie:** Three answers. It belonged to the Pruitts on the north end and it
belonged to a woman at the mill in Hayesville and it belonged to nobody because
there is a whole generation in this valley that does not keep a dog in a fenced
yard, which is a real thing I learned in 2011.

**Player:** Which one.

**Junie:** The mill one is real. Her name is Bobbie Sears and she has had that
dog since she was a girl and she has not come out of her house since the dog.

**Player:** Then why would somebody shoot it on that fence line.

**Junie:** I do not know. I am a schoolteacher, I am not the police, and I have
been at this since Tuesday with two telephone lines and a porch.

**Player:** What if it was an accident.

**Junie:** Then we find that out too and we stop, because the person who fired
does not know they killed somebody's dog either, and the same is true about
fence posts.

**Player:** What if they knew.

**Junie:** Then I have been putting a schoolteacher and a map in front of a
shooting, which is a thing I would like to stop doing before it is a thing
somebody else does.

### Stage 5. The two doors

**Objective:** Bring the plates to both houses. Both doors get opened. What
happens on the porch is decided by what the player says and how long they take.

**At the Sizemore house.** Del has both cups out, which is a thing he has done
once before.

**Del:** That is the 1946 line.

**Player:** It is. It puts the fence forty feet south of where it is, which is
what Ruthie Tolliver told me before I came up here.

**Del:** Then Ruthie Tolliver has been right for sixty years and I have been
wrong, and I would like you to leave before I say anything else, because I am
sixty eight years old and I have not got a second sentence about this in me.

**Player:** Do you want the ground.

**Del:** I have got the ground. I have had the ground since 1946. I want you to
hear the difference between that and wanting it, because if you cannot hear that
difference you are going to be a very useful person for somebody worse than me.

**Player:** The dog.

**Del:** I did not shoot that dog.

**Player:** Was it from your side.

**Del:** I have not been on that fence line since the second of May, which I
know is not a good answer, and I am going to give it to you anyway, because I am
tired of the arrangement where saying anything at all is the same as admitting
everything.

**Player:** Where were you.

**Del:** In Asheville. Two nights. There is a hotel and I can get the receipt
out of the truck and you can look at the date on it, and if you turn that into a
lie about me I will know for the rest of my life and so will you.

**At the Tolliver house.** Ruthie has the plates on her kitchen table with a
magnifying glass and a phone book.

**Ruthie:** Forty feet. Sixty years. And they took that pasture off that creek
in the spring of 1946 with a fence line that was wrong on the day it went in,
and my mother knew it, and my mother never said a word about it to a living soul
except to point at it once.

**Player:** So the first fence was built wrong on purpose.

**Ruthie:** I do not know on purpose. I know somebody was in a hurry and
somebody was angry and they got the line wrong, and then it was too late to fix
it without admitting it had been wrong, and so it stayed wrong, and here we
are.

**Player:** And you have been right since you were nine.

**Ruthie:** I have been angry since I was nine. Those are not the same and I
have had sixty two years of somebody in this county telling me they are.

**Player:** The dog.

**Ruthie:** Now you are going to ask me who I think shot it.

**Player:** I am going to ask it at both houses.

**Ruthie:** Then you already know I am going to name somebody, because that is
what I do instead of sitting here. And I want you to understand that I have a
name and I have had it for six weeks and I have not said it out loud to one
human being, and if you sit in that chair and let me say it, it will exist.

**Player:** Say it.

**Ruthie:** No. Not to you. Not yet.

### Stage 6. Resolution

**Objective:** Finish it. Four endings. The player picks the ending with what
they do, not with what they pick from a menu.

**Ending A, the line gets moved.** The player takes both plats to a county
surveyor who is a stranger and neutral, and pays for the survey out of pocket.

*Both houses are empty when the surveyor leaves. Neither of them will come out.*

**Junie:** It is done, then. Sixty years and it took nine days and a man with a
theodolite.

**Player:** Nobody has to say anything.

**Junie:** They will have to say something. Del has to move a fence in October
and Ruthie has to stand there and watch him do it, and those two people will be
looking at each other over a wire fence for the rest of their lives.

**Player:** It was already broken.

**Junie:** It was already broken. I did not say that to make you feel better.
I said it because the survey does not fix a broken thing, it just finds out how
broken it is, and now everyone in this valley knows, and there is no version of
the next ten years where that is comfortable.

**Player:** Who shot the dog.

**Junie:** Del's grandson. He was sixteen and he was up there because he was
told to be somewhere else and he shot it by accident with a rifle he should not
have had, and Ruthie Tolliver figured it out in eleven days and has not said a
word, and Del knew it in April and has not said a word.

**Player:** Then it is an accident.

**Junie:** It is an accident that has been held over this town by two people who
would rather bury a dog than a reason. You can decide for yourself what that
means. I have been living here forty years and I still do not have a word for
it, and I taught eighth grade, which is a job where you have to have a word for
everything by June.

**State:** unrest down in Mountain Rest, boundary settled, both family relations
at exactly where they started. Neither family is grateful. Both are quieter.

**Ending B, the player takes a side and forces the issue.** The player backs Del
publicly, using the money and the muscle, and the 1946 plate never gets filed.

**Del:** You did not have to do that.

**Player:** No.

**Del:** That is not what I meant and I am too tired to do it again. What I meant
is that nobody has ever come up this lane and asked me one question in twelve
years, and you asked me two on a Tuesday and you did not like either answer, and
now there is a fence that is going up on Friday that is in the right place.

**Player:** Ruthie is going to fight it.

**Del:** Ruthie is going to fight it with a lawyer and a magnifier and a thing
her mother told her once when she was nine, and she is going to be right about
the history and wrong about the ground, and I am going to let her do all of it,
because she has earned a fight. I have not earned one and I am not going to take
it from her.

**Player:** You just did.

**Del:** Yeah. Well.

**State:** unrest up sharply for one quarter, then down hard as soon as one side
has nothing to organize around. loyalty up in Mountain Rest, which is the ugliest
state write in this file and the Why panel has to let the player read it.

**Ending C, the player takes Ruthie's side.** The player produces the 1946
plat, files it, and lets the record do the work. The fence comes down and
nothing replaces it.

**Ruthie:** Sixty years. And I am the one standing here with a bare yard.

**Player:** That is what the document says.

**Ruthie:** The document says a fence line. It does not say what a person does
with a bare yard for the next thirty years, and Del is seventy one and he has
that pasture in his blood and he cannot look at that creek without somebody
reminding him he lost it, and I will be reminding him, every Sunday, for the rest
of my life.

**Player:** You could have asked for a different line.

**Ruthie:** I could have asked for a different line in 2019 and I could have
asked for a different line every year after that, and I want you to know that
every single time I did not, and you know why, because you have been up that
lane.

**Player:** Because a fence is something to be angry about.

**Ruthie:** Because a fence is something to be angry about and a bare yard is
something to be sad in, and I am sixty two and I have my choice and I made it a
long time before you got here.

**State:** loyalty up with the Tollivers, down with the Sizemores, and Ruthie
Tolliver stops being a notable and becomes an ordinary woman with a bare yard,
which the game must treat as a real ending and not a punishment.

**Ending D, the plates stay in the tin.** The player does not file anything.

**Junie:** I am not going to say anything and I would like you to go home.

**Player:** I could file it.

**Junie:** You could. And you have decided not to, which is a decision, and I am
a schoolteacher, I know what a decision looks like.

**Player:** What happens to the dog.

**Junie:** Nothing happens to the dog. Nothing happens to Del's grandson either,
because he is sixteen and he is somebody's grandson and he has not been in
trouble for anything in his life, and if this goes the other way he will be in
trouble for everything for the rest of it.

**Player:** Somebody else shoots a person.

**Junie:** Then this becomes two families with a night each. That is what I told
you on this porch and I told you before I knew anything, and you came back
anyway.

**State:** unrest up, one family is gone from the town by December and is a
settlement of nine households elsewhere, loyalty mixed, and Junie keeps both
plats in the tin because she has never once in her life thrown anything away.

### Notes for implementation

- The dog is the engine of this chain and it must never be resolved cleanly. If
  the player walks away from the dog question, the game should let them, and the
  Why panel should say what that cost.
- Ruthie Tolliver is the most competent person in this file and she is not
  morally right. Do not let dialogue sort her into a good column.
- Del Sizemore is not the villain of the feud. Neither is Ruthie. The villain of
  this chain, if it has to be one, is the arrangement where both of them would
  rather lose than be the one who moved.
- Ending B must be written so that the player cannot feel good about it. The
  unrest number does that, not a line of dialogue.
