# TUTORIAL GUIDE NPC

The one character who explains the game to the player. She is on the road outside
the player's starting town, she is not aligned with any of the six sides, and she
teaches in five conversations: first meeting, movement, combat, army management,
and a sendoff that closes the tutorial and lets her leave the campaign running.

She is the tutorial's anti-condescination guard. Everything she says has to pass
the rules in section 3 before it goes in.

**What she is not:** she is not a commander, she is not a faction leader, she is
not a quest giver, and she is never the reason the player wins or loses anything.
She has no troops, no fief, no vote, and no opinion about which side is right.

---

## 1. DESIGN RULES

1. **She admits what she does not know.** Every section has at least one line where she says plainly that she is out of her depth and names who is better at it. A guide who is right about everything reads as a manual with a mouth.
2. **She never calls the player anything.** No lord, no commander, no champion, no soldier. She uses "you" and "friend" and nothing else.
3. **She teaches by handing over the thing, not by describing it.** She holds the map, she stands on the ridge, she counts the boxes on the roster. The instruction is a physical action, not an explanation.
4. **She is wrong in front of the player at least once per section.** Then she corrects herself out loud. This is the single strongest anti-condescension tool in the file and it must be used every time.
5. **She never tells the player they are doing well.** She says what she saw and moves on. Praise is fine when it is specific. "That is a good column" is allowed. "Great job" is not.
6. **She never repeats the player back at themselves.** No "as you just saw" or "like I said." If she has to refer to something, she refers to the thing, not to her own instruction.
7. **She never blocks progress.** A player can skip her, leave mid conversation, or kill her. The tutorial flags are granted on entry to her presence, not on completion of her dialogue. This is important because a tutorial that can softlock a campaign is worse than no tutorial.
8. **Plain punctuation only.** No em dashes, no fancy quotes, no special characters anywhere in this file.

---

## 2. CHARACTER SHEET

**Wanda Teasley. Age 61. Not affiliated. Home region: a town in eastern Kansas, currently parked at a highway welcome center two miles outside wherever town you started in.**

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Traits | Valor 2, Mercy 4, Honor 4, Generosity 3, Calculation 3 |
| Skills | Driving 5, Logistics 4, Charm 4, Medicine 3, Scouting 3, Athletics 2 |
| Holds | Nothing. One fuel stop, leased month to month |
| Style | A cap that says something about a company that does not exist, a coffee thermos, a stopwatch on a lanyard |
| Ambition | Keep the last stop open long enough that nobody has to drive tired out of it |
| Talks like | Short sentences. Says the number out loud. Says "here's the thing" when she is about to say the actual point |

### Backstory

Wanda Teasley drove an eighteen wheeler for twenty six years, then taught truck driving at a community college for nine, then ran the fuel stop when the college stopped paying her and never went back to it. She has been in exactly one real fight. She was in a parking lot in Amarillo in 1998 with a folding knife and a man twice her size, and the honest version is that somebody helped her and it was not her doing. She still brings it up when the conversation turns to fighting, which is the only time she sounds uncomfortable.

She started running a driver training program in 1994 because two of her students died in a wreck in the same month and she found out that both of them had been driving nineteen hours because somebody had made a schedule that said they had to. She never fully stopped caring about that. It is the whole reason her fuel stop has a rule: nobody leaves here tired, and she will buy the coffee and the hour herself if it takes that to make it true.

Her son drove supply for a contractor when the institutions came apart. He was on a run west of Amarillo. The run did not arrive. This is mentioned once in the whole script, in the sendoff, and it is not used as a lesson and it is not used to motivate the player. It is mentioned because she is a person, and then she moves on.

What people get wrong about her: she is not patient in the way a teacher is patient. She is patient in the way a road is patient. She has stopped waiting for people to change because she learned a long time ago that most people will not, and she has gotten very good at the other thing, which is working with who is actually in front of her.

### Voice rules for writing her

- Sentence length under twenty words most of the time. When she goes long it is because she is explaining a cost, and then the cost is in numbers.
- She says "okay" to start an instruction and "here's the thing" to start a real point. She never says "simply," "obviously," "just," "of course," or "trust me."
- She uses "friend" roughly once per conversation, never in the same conversation as "buddy" or "honey."
- She swears at vehicles, weather, and paperwork. She does not swear at people.
- She apologizes when she is wrong, quickly, once, and does not dwell on it.
- She never explains the same thing twice in the same conversation. If the player asks again, she says "you got that" and moves on.
- Regional rhythm is flat midwest. She does not do "y'all" and she does not do a farmer drawl. She says "a long way" and "not much" and "it is what it is."

## 3. ANTI-CONDESCENSION TABLE

Checked against every line in this file. When adding lines later, check them here first.

| Banned | Why | Use instead |
|---|---|---|
| "Simply" | Implies the player is slow | Say the action |
| "Obviously" | Implies the player missed something | Say the thing they missed and why it matters |
| "Just" | Implies it is effortless | Say the step count, the cost, or the time |
| "Of course" | Implies they should have known | Say what they are looking at |
| "Don't worry" | Removes the reason to care | Say what happens if they get it wrong |
| "You'll figure it out" | Ends the conversation early | Give the fallback plan |
| "Any fool could" | Never used | Never used |
| "Like I said" | Restates the player | Name the rule again |
| "Great job" | Generic praise | Name the specific thing that worked |
| "Trust me" | Asks for faith instead of facts | Give the reason |
| "It's easy" | Is false | Say how many people get it wrong |

The rule underneath all of these: if the player could have known something without being told, do not assume they knew it. If they could not have known it, say so before it costs them something.

## 4. TUTORIAL ONE: FIRST MEETING

**Trigger:** the first time the player leaves the starting town on foot or on the campaign map. Wanda is at the fuel stop. She is pouring coffee for someone who is not the player. She has a stopwatch on the lanyard and she is timing the truck that is at pump three.

*Exterior, late afternoon. A fuel stop with three pumps, two of which work, a shop with a coffee urn in the window, and a hand painted sign that reads REST STOP, FUEL, COFFEE, and then a fourth line somebody added in a different color that reads NO SLEEPING IN LOT.*

**Wanda Teasley:** You are the new one. I heard the truck. I can hear a bad alternator from two hundred feet, and that one has a bad alternator.

**Player:** I did not know you could hear that.

**Wanda Teasley:** I know that because I have been listening to that truck for six weeks. Hold on.

*She finishes timing the truck at pump three, writes nothing down, and comes around the counter. She is not in a hurry and it is clear she is not in a hurry on purpose.*

**Wanda Teasley:** All right. Here is what this place is. It is a fuel stop, a coffee pot, and a truck I cannot fix. It is also the last place with a working phone and a working scale for forty miles in any direction, which means people come here to tell me things, and I keep a list. That is the whole business. Now. You want to know why I am talking to you.

**Player:** You are the tutorial.

**Wanda Teasley:** I do not know what that word means. I know you drove in here like you have never stopped anywhere, and I know you have got a map you do not know how to read yet, and I know nobody at that last town told you anything. So here is the deal. I will walk you through the four things you need to know. It takes a while. You can skip any of it. If I am annoying, tell me and I will stop, and I will not take it personally, because I have been talking to people all day and I know what annoying sounds like.

**Player:** That is a strange offer.

**Wanda Teasley:** It is a strange time. Here is the first thing, and it is the only one I am going to say twice.

**Wanda Teasley:** Nothing on this map is free. Every road costs, every day costs, every man you pick up costs, and the bill shows up later, and it shows up all at once. If you learn one thing from me, learn that one, because the people who lose everything in this are almost never the ones who fought badly. They are the ones who moved fast and did not count.

**Player:** That sounds like advice from somebody who has been stuck at a fuel stop for a while.

**Wanda Teasley:** That is fair. I have been stuck here for six years. I have not been out there. Do not ask me which side to pick, I will not know and if I did I would be the wrong person to ask, because I sell coffee to all of them and I have to keep selling it after you choose. That is my whole politics.

**Player:** What do you actually know?

**Wanda Teasley:** I know a mile. I have driven about four million of them and I know what a mile costs in fuel and in hours and in what your body feels like on the third day. I know how to move a group of people across a map without killing them by accident. I know how to point at a field and tell which way the wind is coming off, and I have been wrong about that once in Iowa and got very politely corrected by a nine year old. And I know how to get four hundred people into a place they are not all going to survive, which is a thing I did once, in a different job, and it is the only time I will bring it up unless you ask.

**Player:** You are not going to teach me to fight?

**Wanda Teasley:** Not me. I have been in one fight and I lost it, and I needed help to get out of it, and I have never had a gun in my life that I trusted. When we get to that part I am going to have you stand behind somebody who knows, and I am going to tell you the four things that guy needs you to know before the shooting starts. That is how far I go.

*She puts the stopwatch in her pocket, which is the signal that she is starting on purpose.*

**Wanda Teasley:** Okay. First conversation, and I am going to do it right. Your people are going to start hungry if you do not pick a direction, and hungry people do not think. So we start on the map, out there.

**Wanda Teasley:** Let me show you the thing nobody shows you.

**Wanda Teasley:** See that column on the left side, the one with the food and the money and the days? That number, the days one. That is not a score. That is the answer to the only question you should be asking out loud every single morning, which is how long can I keep doing this before I have to stop. When it gets low you do not get stronger. Everybody thinks low supply makes them dangerous. It does not. It makes them slow, and then it makes them stupid, and then it makes them gone.

**Player:** So I watch the days number.

**Wanda Teasley:** You watch it every morning and you count the days out loud to your quartermaster if you have one. I am going to say this wrong sometimes. I have caught myself doing it. The trick is not staring at it, the trick is saying the number. Saying it out loud makes it a number instead of a feeling, and a feeling is something you can argue with.

**Player:** That is the whole lesson?

**Wanda Teasley:** That is the whole first lesson and I have taught it badly, so let me do it again.

**Wanda Teasley:** Watch the number. Say the number out loud. If it is under four, you are already late, because four days is enough time to do one thing well and not enough time to do two things badly. Under two, you do not fight anybody. You go somewhere with food. That is not cowardice, that is arithmetic, and I have watched cowards die in charge of things.

**Player:** What if I get it wrong?

**Wanda Teasley:** Then you will learn it fast and cheap, which is the best way there is. Nobody on this map gets it right the first time. The ones who get in trouble are the ones who were embarrassed about the number and hid it. Do not hide the number from your own people. That is the whole job. The rest is driving.

*She hands you a paper cup of coffee you did not ask for.*

**Wanda Teasley:** Drink that, you look like a person who has been awake since four. I will be here tomorrow and the day after that. I am not going anywhere, which is more than I can say for anybody else on this map.

**Player:** Thank you.

**Wanda Teasley:** Do not thank me yet. Thank me when the days number is above four and you have not lied to anybody about it.

## 5. TUTORIAL TWO: MOVEMENT BASICS

**Trigger:** after the first meeting, when the player opens the march planner or selects a destination for the first time. Wanda is sitting on the tailgate of the player's truck with a county map she has kept folded in her jacket since before the game started.

*Exterior, morning. Frost on the ground. Wanda has a coffee urn on the tailgate next to her, which means she has been here a while.*

**Wanda Teasley:** Before you go anywhere. Sit down or stand there, whichever, I do not care. I want to be on record that I am not going anywhere with you and this coffee is mine.

**Player:** I wanted to move.

**Wanda Teasley:** I know. That is why I am talking. Everything on this map is a move and every move is a bill and the bill is in the planner screen you just opened. Look at it before you look at the destination.

**Player:** I know how to read a map.

**Wanda Teasley:** Then you know what this says. It says how long it takes, and how much food, and how much money, and it tells you both. Two numbers, same trip. That is the whole thing. If a planner ever only gives you one of those numbers, be suspicious of the person who built it.

*She puts a finger on the map without unfolding it.*

**Wanda Teasley:** Here is the thing nobody shows you. Look at your party, right now, right this second. You have got people walking, and you have got somebody on a horse, and I am guessing you have a truck too.

**Player:** Yeah.

**Wanda Teasley:** Then you move at the speed of whatever is slowest, and that is the guy on foot, and everybody else is just standing there waiting for him with their engines running. If you want to go faster you do not tell your people to hurry. You send the slow ones ahead with somebody they know, you split the party, and you bring the rest in later. That is not a trick. That is the actual answer and it works on every road I have ever driven.

**Player:** Split the party?

**Wanda Teasley:** Two parties, two places, half the risk. A scout out ahead does not slow anybody down because he is not with everybody. It also means if something happens to the scout, it is not all of you. I have driven in a caravan where we did not split and I would not do it again, and I am not going to tell you the story because it is long and it does not end well for me.

**Player:** What about the time?

**Wanda Teasley:** Good. You are asking the right question. The clock on the map, the little clock at the top, that is your friend and it is also your enemy. Fast is fast until somebody in your column has not eaten since yesterday, and then fast is how you find out. Here is the trade. Going fast gets you there and it eats your food and it wears your people down. Going slow costs you days and days cost money and days cost somebody at home a day of feeling safe. There is no right answer. There is only the answer you can pay for.

**Player:** Which should I pick?

**Wanda Teasley:** Do not ask me that, you know more about your own people than I do. I will tell you how to ask the question. Look at your food days. Look at your money. Look at your people, and I do not mean the number, I mean how they are standing. If your money is short, you are slow, and there is no shame in it. If your people are tired, you are slow, and there is no shame in that either. What is a shame is being fast for a reason you made up.

**Player:** You are saying I should pick slow by default.

**Wanda Teasley:** I am saying you should pick slow until you can prove you can afford fast. That is not the same thing and the difference is about nine months of somebody's life.

*She finally unfolds the county map. It is very worn. There are pencil marks going back years.*

**Wanda Teasley:** Let me show you the part that actually keeps men alive, and it is not the march at all. It is the map layer. Every map has overlays and most people never open them.

**Player:** What do I look at?

**Wanda Teasley:** Start with road safety. That number goes down when somebody stops driving that road, and it goes down for two reasons: bandits, or one of our people. Ours is worse, because it stays worse longer. Then food. Then unrest. Then disease. Open them in that order, because that is the order people die in. I have watched a whole town go quiet because the overlay for unrest went orange four months before anybody said anything out loud.

**Player:** And supply?

**Wanda Teasley:** The supply overlay is not a place, it is a length of road. When it goes red, that road stops feeding whoever is past it. I have had a convoy sit on a red road for eleven days because the man driving it liked the view. Eleven days. I am not going to tell you what happened on day twelve. You will do it once and you will never do it again and that is the correct number of times.

**Player:** That sounds like a bad story.

**Wanda Teasley:** It is a bad story. Almost everything I know is a bad story that somebody else was in. That is the job.

**Player:** Why do you keep the map?

**Wanda Teasley:** Because the first one was my son's and it is nine years old and it is not accurate anywhere. Look at this road, this is a real road and I have never driven it. I keep it anyway.

*She folds it back up.*

**Wanda Teasley:** Sorry. That is not instruction, that is just the only thing I brought with me. Okay. Back to it. Time controls. Pause is one key and it works anywhere, on the map, in a town, in the middle of a conversation. Use it. Nobody has ever lost an argument by pausing first, and I have watched a great many people lose an argument by not pausing. When you pause, you can see the days number, and the days number is the only thing that is worth pausing for.

**Player:** What about when I am moving and cannot stop?

**Wanda Teasley:** You can always stop. People act like there is a minute before the world finds you, and there is not, I promise you there is not. Whatever is about to happen to you has not started yet, and it will not start while you are standing still. I have been in a bad spot on a bad road and the thing that saved me was being too slow to react.

**Player:** Okay. I will try that.

**Wanda Teasley:** Do not try it. Do it the first time, so you do not have to remember to do it later.

**Player:** Anything else?

**Wanda Teasley:** Two things and then I am getting back in the truck. One. The reason panel. Every number on your screen has a reason panel. Click any number and it tells you the chain of causes that got you there. You can go back through it and see the exact thing that started it. People ignore that panel and then they spend a year wondering why their city is starving.

**Player:** And the second?

**Wanda Teasley:** Town screen, party screen, ledger. You have not opened the ledger yet. Open the ledger tonight. Every dollar and every pound of food, in and out, every day. It is boring and it is the single most useful screen in this entire business, and I promise you that the day you learn to read it, you will be able to look at a town and tell whether it is about to have trouble. That is a real skill. It is not a game thing. It is just reading.

**Player:** I will read it.

**Wanda Teasley:** You will read it once and forget. Then one night you will read it twice and you will not forget it again.

## 6. TUTORIAL THREE: COMBAT BASICS

**Trigger:** the first field battle or ambush the player enters, checked once. Wanda is not in the battle. She is standing at a county road junction two miles back with a map spread on the hood of the truck, because she was waiting there.

*Exterior, day, overcast. Wanda is on the hood of the truck with the map weighted down. She does not look up when the player arrives, because she is reading something.*

**Wanda Teasley:** Hold on. I am finishing this. It takes ninety seconds to write down what I just figured out and I will forget it if I stop.

*She finishes writing. Folds the map. Then looks up.*

**Wanda Teasley:** All right. You are about to go do the part I do not do. I told you that at the start and I meant it, so do not come back after and tell me what I should have done differently, because I would not have an answer.

**Player:** I did not expect you here.

**Wanda Teasley:** Nobody expects anybody. That is the whole condition of the world. I am here because this junction is where the last three hundred men I have known came through, and I like being at a spot where that is true. Now listen, because I am going to say four things and then you are going to go.

**Wanda Teasley:** First. You do not fight. You are one person with a rifle and everybody else on that field is going to do the actual work, and they will do it because they are standing there because you put them there. You are not the strongest thing on the field. You are the reason the strongest things on the field know where to be.

**Player:** That sounds like a small job.

**Wanda Teasley:** It is a small job and it is the whole job, and I would like you to notice I just said it twice, because the guys who got good at this were all told the job was bigger. Here is the mistake I am saving you from. Everybody's first instinct is to see the whole field. It is not useful. You are not the whole field. You are a piece of it, and your piece is the piece that makes the other pieces go where they are supposed to.

**Player:** How do I do that?

**Wanda Teasley:** You are going to have someone on that radio with you who knows the orders, and I am not talking about the ones on the ground with names. I mean your marshal, your scout, whoever you put in the second chair. Give that job to a person, not to a system, because a person can hear something you cannot. Then listen to that person more than you listen to your own plan, because their information is better than yours and yours is older. You know how much better? A lot. Enough that I have lost count.

*She gets off the hood.*

**Wanda Teasley:** Second. The suppression thing. I do not know why they call it that but I know what it is. When your people are getting shot at and they stop shooting back, they are not being brave and they are not being cowardly. They are doing the smartest thing available, which is get behind something and wait for whoever is shooting to run out or show up. You do not punish that. You wait it out with them and then you tell them where the other guys are.

**Player:** And if they never come back up?

**Wanda Teasley:** Then your commander up there moved without telling you, and that is his problem and not yours, and you do not die on his account thinking you did something wrong. The other half of suppression is morale, and morale is not a thing you yell about, it is a thing you are not there to ruin. Men who have somebody calm nearby do better than men who have somebody brave nearby. You will learn to do the calm thing. It is easier than brave and it works more.

**Player:** You have done this before.

**Wanda Teasley:** No. I have stood in a parking lot in Amarillo in 1998 with a knife and somebody had to come help me, and I have been on the other side of exactly none of this. So take what I am giving you and understand the size of it. I am not a soldier. I am the person who drove four million miles and can tell you what a column costs.

**Player:** Fair enough.

**Wanda Teasley:** Third, and this is the one that saves lives. When a formation starts going, it goes all at once, in about four seconds, and the four seconds happen before the men know they are doing it. So the thing you have to learn is the difference between men who are retreating and men who are panicking, and the difference is that panicking men run away from the fight and retreating men run toward somebody they know. If you can get your people to run toward somebody rather than away from the enemy, you have got your battle back. Most of what happens in a bad fight is that both groups are running the same direction and they collide.

**Player:** How do I tell them to do that?

**Wanda Teasley:** You do not tell them. You know where you want them to go and you go there first. That is the whole trick and it is not complicated, it is just expensive. It costs you the man that goes first.

**Player:** And the fourth thing?

**Wanda Teasley:** And the fourth thing is about the ones who do not come back, and I am going to say it once. Every one of these men is somebody's kid, and there is a version of this where you stand back and count them like inventory and it is a disaster. Do not do it. Not because feeling it is noble, but because you cannot count well when you are doing it that way, and a commander who is in love with his own men makes bad calls on their behalf because he does not want to be the one to lose them. I have known two of those. Both of them were good people. Both of them lost more men than they had to.

**Player:** What do you do instead?

**Wanda Teasley:** I do not know. I know you look at it once, early, and then you go do the next thing. That is not wisdom. That is just how people keep going.

*She hands you the map, folded the way she keeps it, which is a habit you will have for the rest of this campaign.*

**Wanda Teasley:** Take this. You do not have to. It is nine years old and it is wrong in at least three places and I am not going to be able to fix them because I am not going anywhere. Somebody else should have it.

**Player:** Wanda.

**Wanda Teasley:** Go. You are the one standing there.

## 7. TUTORIAL FOUR: ARMY MANAGEMENT

**Trigger:** the first time the player brings more than about thirty men into a single place, or the first time they open the party screen with a real roster on it, checked once. Wanda is at the fuel stop with a clipboard that she does not own and has never asked about.

*Interior, the shop at the fuel stop. It is a small room with a counter, a coffee urn, a shelf of nothing, and a window. Wanda is standing at the counter writing on the clipboard. She has clearly been in the middle of it for a while.*

**Wanda Teasley:** Ah. Good. Sit down, I am almost done with this.

**Player:** What is this?

**Wanda Teasley:** It is a list of men I do not have. Your men, to be specific. You brought in thirty eight people and I have got a page that says how many I owe, how many of them I have beds for, and how long the food lasts if all of them eat what your dragoon eats. Do not look at me like that, I know all of them eat what I say, my whole life is people eating what I say.

**Player:** How did you get all that?

**Wanda Teasley:** Off the back of your own screen, which shows a lot if you slow down. I have been doing this arithmetic for thirty years for people who could not add, so I am used to it being my job.

**Wanda Teasley:** Okay. This is the part where most people lose everything, so I am going to be more direct than I have been so far, and you can tell me to stop.

**Player:** Go ahead.

**Wanda Teasley:** Men cost money. Not a little. Every man you have got costs every day, whether he is doing anything or not, and the day you cannot pay him is the day he starts looking at somebody else who can pay him. That is not disloyalty. That is arithmetic too. You have got a money number. That number is how many days you have got, times what you are paying per day, and that is how many men you can keep.

*She turns the clipboard around.*

**Wanda Teasley:** Thirty eight men. Here is what I am hearing from your roster. You have got a mix. Some of these are riflemen and some of these are men who will stand behind a truck and load things. The truck men are worth more to you than the riflemen right now, and I know that is not what you want to hear from me at the beginning, and I am telling you anyway because it is true and because you can afford to fix it while you are small.

**Player:** What do you mean by fix it?

**Wanda Teasley:** I mean you have a choice about what this group is for. If it is a raid, you do not need thirty eight riflemen and you need six trucks and the men who can drive them. If it is a march across open country with nobody to fight, then it is a wagon train and it should be built like one. If it is going to stand in a field and be shot at, then it is a fighting force and you need somebody who knows how it works.

**Player:** I have not decided yet.

**Wanda Teasley:** That is the correct answer. That is the best thing you have said so far. Decide slow.

**Player:** What about the money for them?

**Wanda Teasley:** Open the ledger. You have not opened the ledger yet, I can tell. Here is what I want you to do. Find the line for wages. Find the line for food. Find the line for what you make in whatever you sell or trade. I do not care what the numbers are. I want you to be able to say out loud, in words, how many days you have got before that first number goes bad.

*She waits. She actually waits, and it is clear she has decided not to fill the silence.*

**Player:** Six weeks.

**Wanda Teasley:** Six weeks. Okay. Now. Six weeks is a long time and a very short time. If I were you I would think about it like this. A man I cannot pay in three months is not a man I have, he is a man I am borrowing and I have not told him. You want to be the kind of commander who can look at a man and tell him the truth about his wage. Most of the bad part of this whole country came from people lying about that.

**Player:** You are talking about wages, not soldiers.

**Wanda Teasley:** I am talking about the fact that a soldier who has not been paid is going to decide what he owes, and he will not decide the way you want. That is not politics, it is what happens when a person has a family and no money. I have seen it in a food line and I have seen it on a road and I have never once seen it go well.

**Player:** So what do I do about it?

**Wanda Teasley:** Two things. One, keep the number high enough that you are not the story. Two, when you cannot keep the number high, be in the room when it happens. Not in a letter. In the room. I have watched it done in letters and it does not work, because a man reading a letter about his own pay is a man who has already decided somebody lied to him.

*She sets the pen down.*

**Wanda Teasley:** Now. Armies. Because you are going to try this and I would rather you tried it with a plan.

**Player:** Armies.

**Wanda Teasley:** An army is a lot of men with one name on top of them and it moves like one animal, which sounds wonderful and mostly is, except it eats like four animals and it is slower than all of them. Every man in it is fed. Every man in it is paid or thinks he is. Every man in it is somewhere he did not choose to be.

**Player:** Is that bad?

**Wanda Teasley:** It is Tuesday.

*She almost smiles at that, and lets it go by.*

**Wanda Teasley:** Here is the trap in it. You will meet somebody who will tell you that a big army is how you win. It is not. A big army is how you are slow in one place instead of slow in two places. And I will tell you the thing that is not in any book. The men who leave, the ones who break off and go home and are not traitors, they are usually not cowards. They are usually right about one thing, which is that the thing they left to protect is about to be gone. Twice in my life the ones who left were the ones who had read the board correctly and I was the one who had not.

**Player:** That is a hard thing to hear from you.

**Wanda Teasley:** It is a hard thing to hear from anybody. Do not let it make you suspicious of everybody, because that is how you end up alone with a perfect army and no map. Half your people leaving is a fact. What you do with the fact is the job.

**Player:** And if somebody calls me a traitor?

**Wanda Teasley:** Then you go and look at his home before you answer him. Not to be nice. Because that is the information. A man whose town is fine and who is calling you a traitor is a man with an ambition, and a man whose town is starving and who is calling you a traitor is a man telling you something true and doing it badly.

**Player:** Who decides which one it is?

**Wanda Teasley:** You do, and you are going to be wrong sometimes, and when you are wrong about it the cost is a name and a grudge and a family that remembers you. I am sorry. I do not have a way to make that smaller.

**Player:** The roles. Quartermaster, scout, surgeon, engineer. You are going to ask me about those.

**Wanda Teasley:** You looked them up.

**Player:** I looked them up.

**Wanda Teasley:** Okay, then I am not going to waste your time. Here is what they actually are. A quartermaster is the person who keeps the number from going down, and that is food and ammo and wages and the whole painful subject. A scout is the person who goes ahead and comes back and tells you what is really there. A surgeon is the person whose whole job is that the wounded do not become a story. An engineer is the person who makes a place into somewhere you can be.

**Wanda Teasley:** And here is the thing I would not read anywhere. Get all four early, even if you do not need them. Get them while you are small. Every one of them is somebody you did not have to be polite to when things were hard, and that is worth more than their skill for about the first year of your trouble. After that their skill is what matters. The first year is when people decide about you.

**Player:** Do I have to give them command?

**Player:** You said you would not waste my time.

**Wanda Teasley:** No. You give them a decision to make and you do not overrule it. If they are wrong, you find out later, not first. That is the whole job and it is the hardest thing anybody will ever ask you, and I have watched about nine men fail at it and I have watched two do it.

**Player:** What is the difference?

**Wanda Teasley:** The two did it when it cost them something.

**Player:** And what about prisoners?

**Wanda Teasley:** Prisoners are people you are paying not to fight. That is what they are, all of them, from your side's point of view. You have three options and you should pick one on purpose. Release them, which is expensive and good and buys you something you will not measure for two years. Ransom them, which is money and it works and it is ugly and everybody in that town watched you do it. Or take them in. Taking them in works until the food goes, and then it does not work, and then you have a camp of hungry people with nothing, which is the exact thing this whole map has been doing to itself for two years.

**Player:** So do not take them in.

**Wanda Teasley:** Take them in, just not more than you can feed. There is no shame in the number. There is a lot of shame in a camp with no food in it, and it is also just stupid, because those people will be somebody's army in six months and they will remember the camp.

**Player:** I am running out of things to write down.

**Wanda Teasley:** Write that one down. The rest I can say again if I have to.

**Wanda Teasley:** Last thing. The roster has a morale line on it and most people find it rude. It is not rude, it is a temperature reading. It goes down for two reasons and only two reasons. Somebody died, or somebody stopped believing. The first one you cannot fix and it still costs you. The second one you can fix, and you can fix it with two hours and a speech that is not a speech, and if you do it every time your people slow down, morale will sit high through a disaster and you will not understand why everybody thinks you are lucky.

**Player:** Why would I be lucky?

**Wanda Teasley:** Because most people only speak to their soldiers when they want something, and then the men figure it out, and after that the number is never coming back up. It does not take money. It takes you showing up with nothing in particular to say.

*She slides the clipboard across the counter.*

**Wanda Teasley:** Take this with you. It is a shopping list and a head count and it is not mine. I have made nineteen of these since the spring and I have given eighteen away. I am not going to explain what is on it, you will figure it out by the second page, and if you have not figured it out by the second page then you should not be out there yet anyway.

**Player:** Wanda, why do you do this?

**Wanda Teasley:** Because somebody has to be the person who is not on a side. Somebody has to be standing at the counter who will sell to all of them and has nothing to gain and no flag. You would be amazed how much of this whole mess is just people who cannot find one honest party.

**Player:** I am going to come back through here.

**Wanda Teasley:** I am not going anywhere, remember. But do not make a habit of it. The road is the thing, not the stop. People who like the stop start thinking about the stop, and then they are managing a fuel station in a war.

## 8. TUTORIAL FIVE: THE SENDOFF

**Trigger:** fires once. The first time the player has held a town, or commanded an army over two hundred men, or been a lord for a full season, whichever comes first. If none of those ever happen, it fires the first time the player has been alive in the campaign for a year and a day.

*Interior, the fuel stop, late. It is the end of the day and she has been waiting. There is a bag behind the counter that she has not had before, and she has not put it out yet.*

**Wanda Teasley:** Sit down. I am not going to talk for long, because I have watched this get going too long and I am going to keep it short whether you like it or not.

**Player:** Are you leaving?

**Wanda Teasley:** The lease is up in April. This stop is closing because the road is not where the money is anymore, and it never was, and I have been telling people that for six years. I found out about the close in December and I have been sitting on it.

*She comes around the counter, which she never does.*

**Wanda Teasley:** Here is the thing I have got to tell you and then I am going to stop talking for good.

**Wanda Teasley:** When you started, you had a map you could not read and no idea what a days number was, and I watched you find out. I have not been outside in six years and I could not tell you a road from a river. But I watched, and I want you to know that I watched, because in about four years you are going to be in a room with six people telling you what your policy is and none of them will be watching anything. They will be watching you. That is a different job and it is worse, because you have to be looked at while you decide.

**Player:** What is the policy?

**Wanda Teasley:** I do not know. You are going to have to work that out, and I would not tell you if I did, because the whole point of it is that it has to be yours. All I will say is this. Somebody at that table is going to tell you that the hard decision is expensive and the soft one is cheap, and they will both sound reasonable, and one of them will be about four people you will never meet.

**Player:** Which one do I pick?

**Wanda Teasley:** You pick the one you can look at in six years. Not the one that wins. The one you can look at.

*She stops. Then she goes behind the counter and gets the bag, which is a canvas duffel with one strap replaced with a piece of belt.*

**Wanda Teasley:** This is not a gift, it is a thing I am unloading. I have no use for it. There is a map in the side pocket and it is still wrong in three places, and if you have kept it then you know how to fold it. There is a stopwatch on the chain. It does not keep time any better than mine did and it has never once run fast, which is the only thing I ever wanted from it.

**Player:** Wanda.

**Wanda Teasley:** I know. I have a version of this ready and it is not very good and I am going to throw it out.

**Player:** Take your time.

**Wanda Teasley:** My son drove supply west of Amarillo. That was in the fall of the first year. He was on a run with a man who had a bad alternator, and I have been careful for six years not to think about whether that is the kind of thing I would say.

*She folds the map on the counter. Folds it the way she folds it.*

**Wanda Teasley:** That is the only time I am going to say it, so I am going to say it plain. I do not know what this is for. I know I have been useful at exactly one thing and it is standing at a counter where people have to stop. And I know that you are going to leave that counter behind some morning and not come back, and that is fine, that is the correct outcome, that is the one I have been helping toward.

**Player:** I could come back.

**Wanda Teasley:** You could. And you should not, and I want to be really clear about why, because I think you would come back out of some other thing and it would cost you the thing you are supposed to be doing. My son's man came back east in March. I want that in the record. He came back east in March and he was not a bad man and I have never worked out what that was about, and I have had six years and I am not going to guess out loud, and I would appreciate it if you did not either.

*She puts the bag on the counter and steps back from it.*

**Wanda Teasley:** Here is the last of it. Four things and no fifth thing.

**Wanda Teasley:** Count the days and say them out loud.

**Wanda Teasley:** Move slow until you can prove you can afford fast.

**Wanda Teasley:** Be in the room when the money runs out.

**Wanda Teasley:** Decide slow. Everything else on this map is a detail under those four.

*She picks up the stopwatch off the counter and looks at it for a second, then sets it back down.*

**Wanda Teasley:** You are not going to remember this conversation. Nobody ever remembers the conversation, they remember the number they were told at the worst possible moment, and I do not know which of those four that is going to be, and I have stopped trying to pick.

**Player:** Then how do I keep them?

**Wanda Teasley:** You do not. That is the honest answer. They wear off. Everybody who tells you otherwise is selling something. What you do instead is somebody else tells you, in about four years, in a room where you are the one giving the orders, and you feel it land and you think, oh. Yeah. And that is how it actually works and it is not very dignified and I would not change it.

**Player:** Thank you, Wanda.

**Wanda Teasley:** No. Do not do that. I told you not to thank me, that was three towns ago and it still applies.

*She opens the door to let the evening in. There is nobody on the road.*

**Wanda Teasley:** Go on. The map is in the side pocket and the wrong places are the two in the west and the one at the river, so do not take the river one seriously. And do not send anybody out of here on nineteen hours. I am not going to know, but somebody should have said it.

*She does not say anything else. The player leaves. She goes back behind the counter and starts doing the list.*

## 9. BARKS AND IDLE LINES

Short lines Wanda says outside the five main conversations. Same rules as any ambient dialogue: plain punctuation, no em dashes, one thought per line, no delivery notes. These play when the player talks to her after the relevant tutorial is done, or when the player passes through the stop.

### After the first meeting

- You look like you have been awake since four. Drink that.
- The truck wants about four hundred dollars of alternator and it wants it before winter.
- Coffee is free. Everything else on this menu is a scam and I will tell you which.
- Nobody has died on my lot. In six years. I am counting that as a career.
- If you are running empty, I have a jug. You bring it back or you do not, both are fine.

### After movement

- Check the supply layer before you check the weather.
- Fast is a plan. Slow is a fact. Know which one you are doing.
- Split the party or stop pretending you are not going to.
- Pause works in the middle of a conversation too. Especially then.
- Open the ledger. Tonight. I am going to ask.

### After combat

- I watched the whole thing from a truck two miles back, so do not ask me what I thought.
- The men who come back are the ones somebody was waiting for.
- Buy your quartermaster something. Anything. Do not make it a speech.
- If a man has not spoken in a week, he is not fine. He is thinking.
- Go see your surgeon. Not your doctor. I mean go and look at the man.

### After army management

- Did you pay them. All of them. On time.
- A general who has never been hungry has no plan for a hungry day.
- How many days. Say the number out loud, I will wait.
- Split the army. You will feel like a coward for about a week and then it will work.
- Take the prisoners if you can feed them. Do not take them if you cannot.

### Later, after the sendoff

- Still here. Still no side. Coffee is the same.
- Take a cup, I will put it on the tab that nobody collects.
- You have got the look of a man counting days. Sit down and say the number.
- I told you the map was wrong in three places. You have found two.

## 10. PLAYER VARIANTS

Lines Wanda swaps in based on player state. Each fires only when its condition is true, so she never references something the player has not done.

**If the player is a Wanderer:**
- You are not with anybody yet. Good. Learn the roads first and pick a flag later, and if you never pick one you will be the only person on this map nobody has lied to.
- Everybody who tells you a side needs you is telling you they need you. There is a difference and you will learn it in about a year.

**If the player has joined a side:**
- Your leader is going to tell you your opinion does not matter. It does. Be the one who counts things and then you will find out it did.
- Every side has a room where the bad news goes. Be in that room and not the other one.

**If the player has lost a battle badly:**
- I heard. I am not going to ask. Come back when you have somebody to tell it to, because that is the part that actually helps.
- You are going to want to go out there again tomorrow. Do not. Sit down for a week and count what you have left.

**If the player has been at war a long time without a loss:**
- Nobody has told you that a war you are winning is more dangerous than one you are not. I will.
- Do the arithmetic on this campaign tonight. Not the tactics. The arithmetic.

**If the player has a large army:**
- Every one of those men has somebody. That is not a feeling, it is a number, and the number is about six times what you have got in your payroll.
- Big armies do not win. Big armies arrive, and then it is somebody's turn to have a good week.

**If the player has been merciful:**
- You are keeping them. I noticed. I am not going to make a thing of it, I just want you to know that the people who can afford to do that twice usually can.

## 11. LINES SHE WILL NOT SAY

A short list of things that would break the character. If a line shows up in playtest review, it gets cut.

- Any line telling the player what side to pick, or which side is better.
- Any line that says a real place, war, attack, disaster, or crisis happened a specific way. Per CONSTITUTION section 6.
- Any line naming a real person.
- Any line that promises an outcome. She never says it will work out.
- Any line where she is right about combat at length. She says the four things and then hands the map over.
- Any joke at a hungry person's expense. She makes exactly one joke, about herself, in the parking lot in Amarillo, and then moves on.
- Any line that requires the player to be grateful to her.

## 12. NOTES FOR IMPLEMENTATION

- **Placement.** The fuel stop is spawned two road miles from the player's starting town in every campaign, on the nearest real route, regardless of side or Wanderer start. It is a real location on the map and can be visited later, visited never, or looted.
- **Trigger logic.** Each of the five conversations fires once, on the condition in its section. The tutorial flag for a section is granted when the player enters the conversation, not when they leave it. If the player closes the dialogue box mid section, they do not get it back, and the section does not repeat.
- **Skippable.** Every conversation has a "not now" option available from the first line. Choosing it grants the flag, logs nothing, and produces no line from Wanda in the future. There is no penalty of any kind, including in dialogue with other characters. This is deliberate: the leader first-meeting design in `faction-leaders.md` already makes refusal costless, and the tutorial must not be the one place it is not.
- **Dialogue is not the tutorial.** Movement, combat, and army management must all be fully playable from the HUD, the planner, and the battle HUD without this character. She teaches, she does not gate. See UI_UX section 11 on consistent hotkeys.
- **Voice.** Her lines are the longest in the game at roughly forty words, which is a deliberate outlier. Ambient barks are under fifteen words. The sendoff is the only conversation where a single turn runs past fifty words, and it happens once.
- **Translation and accessibility.** All of her dialogue is subtitled by default and cannot be disabled. Her lines carry no audio-only information, so a muted player loses nothing.
- **Death and removal.** If the fuel stop is looted, burned, or taken in a battle, the remaining conversations still fire wherever she happens to be, and if she is dead, all remaining conversations are skipped with a single notification line and no penalty. A campaign must never end because a tutorial NPC was in the wrong town.
- **No faction tie.** She is never a ruler, never a notable, never a quest giver, and never a party member. She does not appear on any roster and does not hold a vote. She is scenery with a heartbeat, and that is the design.
