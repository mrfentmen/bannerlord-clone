# ANTAGONISTS

The six people who stand between the player and a quiet career. They are not faction leaders. They are the other kind of leader: the ones who took the same break the player did, made a different decision, and now own the thing the player wants.

Design rules these follow:

- One per section, and each one attacks the exact weakness its own section leader admits to in `faction-leaders.md`. Vega cannot feed the coast, so someone else decided to own the water. Grant cannot project force, so someone else bought the passes. Oyelaran will not sell grain out from under a governor, so one did. Jessup holds the south together with personal warmth, so there is a man out there who does it better. Adair cannot hold two places at once, so somebody else owns the road between them. Castille has the paper, so somebody else has the argument.
- Fictional people, real places. No real person, party, movement, or organisation, per CONSTITUTION.md section 6.
- Every one of them can be fought, bought, joined, beaten in court, or talked down. Every one of them is wrong about one thing, and that is where the content is.
- Traits and skills use the vocabulary from RULERS.md sections 3 and 4 and CHARACTER.md section 3, so an antagonist can be scored by the same code that scores a generated lord.
- Dialogue is plain spoken American English. Contractions, fragments, and the regional rhythm of the section. No one speaks in a briefing document.

## 1. REY ALCARAZ

**Pacific Compact rival. Age 58. Home region: Kern County, California, now based in Bakersfield.**

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Traits | Valor 2, Mercy 1, Honor 2, Generosity 1, Calculation 5 |
| Skills | Logistics 5, Trade 4, Leadership 4, Streetcraft 4, Engineering 3 |
| Holds | Bakersfield, four pumping stations, 190,000 acres of orchard and field |
| Style | A pressed short sleeve shirt in February, reading glasses he uses to look at a page and not at you |
| Ambition | Be the man the coast comes to when the coast runs dry |
| Talks like | Allocations. He thinks out loud in percentages, tonnages, and days, and he does that because it makes people stop arguing with him |

### Backstory

Rey Alcaraz was a field man for a fruit cooperative that got crushed in a bad year, when the co-op could not make payroll for its pickers and the packing lines went quiet for eleven weeks. He watched the water come out from people who had land, which is the part of the story he tells, and then he spent the next eighteen years buying the water, which is the part of the story that is actually true.

He bought his first pumping station inexpensively, from a family that had lost a well to a dry year and could not argue with the acreage. He has bought six since. Nobody stopped him, because the permits were legal, the money was his, and every county up the San Joaquin wanted the tax base. He owns the wells now the way Castille owns paper, and he has the same relationship to every government in his section: courteous, patient, and impossible to ignore.

He does not hate the coast. That is the part that makes him hard. When the coastal cities ration, he sells them water at a price that is fair, and when a coastal city complains, he can point at a receipt showing that he kept them alive at his own cost. He has never fired a shot at anybody. He has ended three communities.

What people get wrong about him: they assume he is a landowner. He is a logistics man who happens to own the taps, and he thinks about water the way Vega thinks about trucks, which is as flow, timing, and reserve. He has read her numbers. He knows her eleven days of grain is really a nine-day problem once you price the inland counties.

### What makes him dangerous

- He does not need to win a battle. He controls the input that decides every battle, and he can turn it off in an afternoon.
- He is the best logistics mind on the west coast and he is using it against the coast, so every supply lesson the player learns from him is real and useful and also a scouting report.
- He is patient past anything the player can afford. He has been buying the section for six years and he never once had to hurry.
- He is not a liar. He says out loud exactly what he is going to do, weeks in advance, in writing, so that later nobody can call him a monster. Half his power is that he gives you notice.

### Threat monologue

*Delivered at a pumping station outside Bakersfield, in front of his crews, with a visitor standing there who is about to be told the schedule. He does not raise his voice once.*

**Rey Alcaraz:** You want to know what I do. Alright. I do not take towns. I do not need towns. I have been out here since before you had a rank and I have never once cared who won an election.

**Rey Alcaraz:** Here is the schedule. Beginning the first of the month I am cutting the orchard allocations for the coastal counties by twelve percent. Not zero. Twelve. That is a real number and it is the whole trick, because twelve percent makes everybody argue about whether it is fair instead of asking why it happened at all.

**Rey Alcaraz:** Then in the second month I cut the field allocations too. Then in the third month I stop selling to the two counties that have been most polite to me, because I want everybody watching those two counties and not watching me.

**Rey Alcaraz:** By the fourth month there is no violence. There is no army. There is a meeting in Los Angeles where eleven people are deciding which one of them gets to be the one who did not need the water. That meeting is where your war gets decided, and I will not be in the room, and I will be the reason for everything said in it.

**Rey Alcaraz:** You think I am a monster because of this. Here is what I actually am. I have watched this section starve twice with the water right there in the ground, and both times the reason was not that there was not enough water. The reason was that nobody in charge decided who got it, and everybody got a little, and a little is nothing.

**Rey Alcaraz:** So somebody decided. Me. I decide who gets it, and I charge a fair price, and I pay my taxes, and I have never fired a shot. If your captain general wants to hang me for that, let him bring a rope and a good horse. I will be working.

### Confrontation

*The player finds him at the third station, a low concrete building with a good roof and a generator that is overkill. There are thirty of his people here and they are all working. He does not stop working when the player walks in. He talks with his hands full.*

**Rey Alcaraz:** You drove from the coast. That is a long drive to be angry at somebody. Sit down, no, stand, I do not care, the point is I am glad you came in person because the letter would have been insulting to both of us.

**Player:** You are starving four counties.

**Rey Alcaraz:** I am charging four counties. There is a difference and you know there is a difference, because you have done it yourself.

**Player:** They cannot pay your price.

**Rey Alcaraz:** They are not paying my price. Vega is paying a credit price and she is guaranteeing it, which means the coast is eating on my risk and her signature, and she is very good at it and she will be wrong about it by winter. I am not starving anybody. I am the only person in this state who would notice.

*He sets down the wrench, which from Rey Alcaraz is the equivalent of a man removing his hat.*

**Rey Alcaraz:** Now I am going to say the part that sounds like a confession and it is not. I have water for about four hundred thousand people right now and about eleven million on this coast, and I have been doing the arithmetic on that gap every day since 2019. So. Tell me your answer. What do you do with eleven million people and four hundred thousand people worth of water? Go ahead, take your time, I have cut a call back open anyway.

**Player:** Open the stations. All of them.

**Rey Alcaraz:** At what price.

**Player:** At any price. Free.

**Rey Alcaraz:** *Then you have killed about nine hundred thousand people in six months and you did not have to fire a shot, and I will not be the one who tells you so, somebody else will, and it will be worse coming from them. Here is what free costs: the water goes to whoever is loudest, the loudest are the biggest cities, and the inland counties are empty in August. I am not being cruel, I am being arithmetic.*

**Player:** You are enjoying this.

**Rey Alcaraz:** I am fifty eight years old and I have been selling water since before your father was a voter and I have never once enjoyed it. What I enjoy is being the only person in California who knows the answer before it becomes a question.

*He picks the wrench back up. The interview is over and he has decided it was.*

**Rey Alcaraz:** I am going to keep watering the coast and I am going to keep charging for it and one day somebody with a rifle is going to decide that is a problem to be solved with a rifle. I hope you get there first. I genuinely do. Go on, take the road back, it is nicer at night.

**Player:** And if I come back with soldiers?

**Rey Alcaraz:** Then I will sell them water too, at a civilian rate, because they drink. That is the other thing about water. Everybody always forgets that the army is a city that moves.

### Notes for implementation

- His defeat paths: cut station power and hold the wells for a month, outbid him on pumps and permits, or turn the coastal governors against his credit price with evidence. The third is the best one and the hardest.
- He is the antagonist who teaches the player Logistics and Tactics while being the reason the player's city is hungry. Every supply skill he demonstrates is a scouting report.
- Weakness to expose: he cannot conceive of a loyalty he did not purchase, so a player who is genuinely loyal to somebody is invisible to him and that is the only way in.

## 2. CORBIN ASHBY

**Mountain Alliance rival. Age 61. Home region: Elko, Nevada, now based out of a trailer park on the Utah line.**

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Traits | Valor 4, Mercy 2, Honor 3, Generosity 3, Calculation 4 |
| Skills | Engineering 5, Leadership 4, Heavy Weapons 4, Tactics 3, Trade 2 |
| Holds | Elko, three pass forts, the only working shaft at the bottom of the mountain |
| Style | A hard hat, a bad knee, and a laugh you can hear through a door |
| Ambition | Own the only way down, so that owning the mountain becomes optional |
| Talks like | Tonnage, grade, and weather. He tells mining stories to explain politics and he does not realize he is doing it |

### Backstory

Corbin Ashby ran underground for twenty-six years, same as a great many men in that part of the country, and he came up with the two things that separate a foreman from a mine owner: he knows which shafts are still profitable, and he does not care about the men who say a shaft is done.

He bought the pass forts in a bad year for the state, three of them, for cash, from a contractor who could not make a payroll. That is the entire purchase. He did not take a fort, he bought one, and he has been very clear and very loud about the difference, because that is the sentence he uses on anybody who suggests he is a warlord.

He pays better than Denver. That is not a boast, it is the mechanism. He pays on the first of the month and pays the full wage in an era where everybody is three weeks late, and the mine crews near his passes have quietly stopped taking Alliance contracts. Grant has the mountains and the mines and no reach past them, and Ashby has the doors. When a man in Winnemucca asks who is running the west, the answer is not a title, it is a pay stub.

He has never pretended to be a patriot. He says openly that he wants to run three counties his way until the passes hold and then he will sell the whole thing to whoever offers most, which unsettles everybody in the Alliance including Grant, because a traitor you can predict is not the problem, a loyal man with your gates is.

### What makes him dangerous

- He owns the high ground that the Mountain Alliance's whole defensive plan depends on, and the Alliance cannot project force out of the mountains anyway. He does not have to beat Grant, he only has to hold the doors and let the section starve slowly.
- He is a better engineer than anybody in the game. Every fort, every shaft head, every road he has improved, is a thing the player can use and also a thing the player can be trapped by.
- He pays well and pays on time, which is the cheapest and most effective loyalty system in the game.
- He does not want to be king. He wants to be the toll keeper on the road to something. Kings are targets, toll keepers are infrastructure, and he has figured that out.

### Threat monologue

*Delivered to his own crews at a hard hat meeting by a fire pit in the cold, with a visitor standing at the edge of the light.*

**Corbin Ashby:** Here is what I want you to understand. Every one of you is paid more this year than you were paid in the last three years combined and I did that without asking Denver for a dime, and I did it because I own the road.

**Corbin Ashby:** Denver is honest. I want to say that first because it is going to sound like I am lying. Hollis Grant has never stolen from a working man in his life and if you ask him for a favor he will drive out himself and do the job. That is the truth. And it has never once gotten him a shaft.

**Corbin Ashby:** They tell me in Boise, in Salt Lake, in Phoenix, everybody is a free man and the mines belong to the section. Fine. Then tell me who maintains the road, and when the answer is nobody, tell me why I should pay a man to clear a rock that his own section will not pay him to clear.

**Corbin Ashby:** I am not going to take this country. Listen, because that is the sentence the wrong man in Denver has been saying about me for four years. I am not taking the country. I am taking the three doors. And you do not need a country to feed a country, you need three doors and a couple of hundred men who own their rifles, and I have got three doors and about two hundred men whose paychecks come from me.

**Corbin Ashby:** In the spring I am raising the gate fee. Not on the section, on everybody. Two hundred a load, cash, and if they do not like it they can go around through the desert, which is four days and a truck radius, and I have watched a man try it and I have watched him come back. And then in the summer I am going to sit down with whoever has the biggest army and sell him the pass for cash, because the pass is mine, and I will sell it to a man I have never met and I will sleep fine, and if one of you thinks that is betrayal, you are welcome to go tell him yourself, and I will write down your name first so I can remember it.

**Corbin Ashby:** I am not going to promise you any flag. I am going to promise you the first of the month. Everything else in this country is somebody's flag and mine is just money.

### Confrontation

*The player meets him at the top pass, where a new concrete pad has been poured over what used to be gravel. Ashby is sitting on a tailgate with his bad leg out in front of him, which he does on purpose so that his men see him hurt and see that he does not hide it.*

**Corbin Ashby:** You are the one who came up the road with the survey flags. You are going to want to talk about the flags. I want to talk about the road.

**Player:** You bought three forts in a bad year.

**Corbin Ashby:** I bought three forts in a bad year. That is all true. I want to correct one thing on the way in, though. Everybody says I took them. Took them from who? From a man who owed me two hundred and forty thousand and could not make a payroll on the eleventh. I paid him in full. I want you to sit with that for a minute, because it is the only thing I have ever done that everybody could check.

**Player:** It does not matter who you paid.

**Corbin Ashby:** It matters enormously, it is just not to you. You are a soldier, so a road is a wall to you and you think owning a wall is being a tyrant. Try owning a wall you cannot pay for and watch what happens.

*He stands up, which costs him, and does not hide it.*

**Corbin Ashby:** All right. Let me tell you what I actually want, because I have got about six minutes before my men think I am being seduced. I want the section to pay me. That is it. That is the whole ask. I will maintain all three passes, all year, at my cost, and I will give Denver the gates back the day the day book balances.

**Player:** The day book.

**Corbin Ashby:** The day book, the road book, the mine book. Whichever one you like. The point is that Grant will not do it, because he cannot get nine governors to agree to pay for anything, and because a man who admits he cannot do it has to hand it to somebody who can. I am not trying to break the Alliance. I am the thing that happens when the Alliance asks a man for more than it can pay for.

**Player:** Say that in front of a governor and see how it lands.

**Corbin Ashby:** I have. Governor Ferris out of Reno heard almost exactly that sentence in August and he did not report me, and I know why he did not report me, because his own crew drives through my gate at four in the morning and gets paid. Do you understand what your problem with me is? You do not have a single person in that section who would be worse off if I disappeared. That is not a thing you can fix with soldiers. That is a thing you fix by making me worse off, and I have been expecting that for four years.

*He gets back on the tailgate. It takes him a second.*

**Corbin Ashby:** Here is the offer, then, since you are not going to ask. Take the pass. Run it your way. I will take a percentage and a chair on whatever board you invent, and I will be a boring, reliable, taxable member of your section. Do it and I will sign it today and sleep like a rock.

**Player:** And if I say no?

**Corbin Ashby:** Then in the spring I raise the gate fee and in the summer I sell the top one to whoever you are least afraid of. I told you already. I do not have to beat anybody, I have to be inconvenient, and being inconvenient is a job I am extremely good at.

**Player:** You are not a warlord.

**Corbin Ashby:** No. I am a man who owns a road. I have thought about that a great deal.

### Notes for implementation

- His defeats: pay the crews better than he can (a real Logistics and money fight), take the top pass by force while he is away at the shaft, or get the section to actually fund road maintenance, which is a quest about the Alliance's own leadership problem.
- He is the antagonist who makes Engineering 5 and Heavy Weapons feel like real estate. The pass forts should be playable terrain with a generator, a fuel problem, and a firing line.
- Weakness to expose: he genuinely does not care who wins, which means he cannot be loyal and can be bought by literally anyone. A player who understands that can turn him.

## 3. WENDELL PREWITT

**Great Lakes Union rival. Governor of Nebraska, age 47. Holds Omaha and the state's grain book.**

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Traits | Valor 2, Mercy 2, Honor 1, Generosity 2, Calculation 5 |
| Skills | Trade 5, Diplomacy 4, Streetcraft 5, Logistics 3, Leadership 3 |
| Holds | Omaha, the state's private elevator capacity, a seat on every regional board |
| Style | A good suit, badly slept in. He offers you coffee before he tells you what he wants |
| Ambition | Be the governor who was right, and be governor of something larger afterward |
| Talks like | Basis and futures. He speaks in numbers with decimal points, because the decimal points are where the lying happens |

### Backstory

Wendell Prewitt was a cooperative banker in Grand Island and then a state official and then a governor, and along the way he did the thing that made him powerful: he never once said out loud that Nebraska deserved better than a queue at somebody else's elevator.

He studied Oyelaran for six years before he broke her. He was not a whirlwind. He waited for a bad basis year, he had his own storage ready, and he opened the export market through a lender in Chicago and a shipping contract in the Pacific, both of which were somebody else's idea, and he let Nebraska look like it was the one that finally got a fair deal. That is his whole method. He is a great man who has never once been the one holding the weapon.

He has broken her single promise, I will never sell your grain out from under you without telling you first, and he did tell her first, technically, in writing, ninety days ahead, which is the detail he brings up whenever anyone accuses him. He has never broken his word. That is why he is dangerous. He has used an honest system to do a dishonest thing and the system is now on his side because it works.

What people get wrong: he is not greedy, he is worse, he is competent and he has a real case. Nebraska has been the Union's grain bin for a century and has never once been a partner, only a supplier, and he has the receipts, and the receipts are not his fault.

### What makes him dangerous

- He fights with paperwork, and the Pacific Compact has the best intelligence on this map, so if the player wants to see his real numbers they need Vega, which means going through a woman who hates him on principle.
- He is the only antagonist who can be stopped without a battle. Court, a council vote, or a governor's meeting will do it, which means the fight against him is a fight about evidence and timing.
- He pays on time and keeps every promise, so he is extremely hard to characterize as a traitor. Vanguard and Mountain lords who could be persuaded will take his side first.
- He is the natural buyer of anything the player wants to sell and the natural lender to anyone desperate, so he ends up owning the player's debts, the player's town, or both.

### Threat monologue

*Delivered in a hotel conference room booked under a cooperative name, to eleven people who could each end his career, none of whom ever will.*

**Wendell Prewitt:** Let me put a number on the table first, because I would rather we start with a number than with a feeling. Nebraska is a hundred and thirty percent of what it was in 2019. Not of our production. Of our income per acre. That is a real statistic and I got it honestly.

**Wendell Prewitt:** I did not steal it. I want that said out loud in this room, because the rumor is that I stole it, and if it gets out that I stole it then all of us are going to be defending an accusation instead of running a state. Look at the year I moved. Look at the basis that winter. Look at who had storage ready and who did not. I was not clever, I was early.

**Wendell Prewitt:** Now. Here is what I have actually done, and I will say it slowly, because I would like you to write some of it down. First, the export market through the coast is open to Nebraska and priced at a floor that our own section cannot match. Second, our share of the Union's reserve is now sold forward through the next three years, which means if there is a bad harvest we do not feel it, and if there is a bad harvest in Illinois or Iowa, those states will feel it and will come to me, because I will be the only governor in this section with grain in his hands and a price he set.

**Wendell Prewitt:** Third, and this is the part that offends our good friend in Chicago, I have not lied to her once. Not once in six years. Every sale went ninety days ahead of schedule. Every contract was public. She can call me on the phone at any hour and read me the terms I sent her myself.

**Wendell Prewitt:** Here is the part I have never said in a public room, so I am saying it to you, and you should not repeat it. She will lose the Union. Not soon, and not obviously, but the day she has to choose between feeding Chicago and honoring a promise to Nebraska, she will feed Chicago, because Chicago is eight million people and Nebraska is two, and I am not insulting her, I am agreeing with her. And when that day comes, the only state in this section with grain is mine, and the governor of Nebraska will be whoever owns that grain.

**Wendell Prewitt:** You can call it a secession if you like. I have never used the word. I have used the word "alignment" and I have used the word "capacity" and I have put both of them in writing.

**Wendell Prewitt:** I am not going to raise an army. I would like you to understand the advantage. An army can be beaten on a Tuesday. What I am building cannot be beaten on a Tuesday, and I will be perfectly honest with you, I am not sure it can be beaten at all.

### Confrontation

*The player gets the meeting by asking for it through a cooperative credit union, which is a real tactic in this game and Prewitt respects it enough to attend. He offers coffee before he sits down.*

**Wendell Prewitt:** Thank you for coming up. I want you to know that I asked for this meeting and not the other way around, because I would like the record to show that I am not afraid of you. Sit, please, that chair is better than the one you are in.

**Player:** You sold the Union's reserve.

**Wendell Prewitt:** I sold Nebraska's share of the Union's reserve. The Union does not have a reserve, it has eleven states with different opinions and one woman in Chicago who is fond of all of us, which is a wonderful thing for her and a disaster for anybody who wants to plan past March.

*He sets the cup down without drinking it.*

**Wendell Prewitt:** Now. You have come to ask me about Bernice. Everyone comes to ask me about Bernice. I am going to tell you the part that makes me look terrible and the part that makes me right, in that order, and then I am going to offer you something, because I have a job that I cannot fill from Nebraska.

**Player:** Which part makes you look terrible?

**Wendell Prewitt:** The look on her face at the February council. She read my letter, which was ninety days ahead, and she read it correctly, and she looked at me for about four seconds. I have thought about those four seconds more than I have thought about anything else in six years. That is the terrible part. She could not say a single thing, because I did it all correctly.

**Player:** And the part that makes you right?

**Wendell Prewitt:** Nebraska put a hundred and thirty percent of a tenth of America through our elevators in twenty years and got thanked for it at a funeral. She promised never to sell us out from under her. She kept it. And keeping it is exactly how you end up a supplier for as long as you live, and I have two daughters and I have watched what that word did to my father.

**Player:** Then surrender. Go back to the Union. Take a smaller share.

**Wendell Prewitt:** No. And I want to explain why, because everyone hears no and stops listening. If I surrender, the shares go back down to what they were, and that is not neutral, that is a second ten percent loss in ten years and eventually there is nothing left to govern. Surrender is not keeping Nebraska safe. It is spending the position to feel clean. I would rather be the man who was right and lost.

*He leans forward. This is the part he actually wants to say.*

**Wendell Prewitt:** So here is the offer, and it is a serious one and I would like an answer this month. You take the governor's seat in a state where you actually live, or you take my contract and run this. I need somebody in Illinois. Illinois has been buying quietly for two years through four intermediaries and I have found all four and I know all four names, and if that gets out I lose about a fifth of my position, so I would like somebody of yours in that state before the governor's commission meets.

**Player:** You are asking me to be a traitor.

**Wendell Prewitt:** I am asking you to do bookkeeping, which is what actually collapses governments, and I would like it noted that I said that out loud. I am not asking you to raise arms. I am asking you to hold a pen for six months.

**Player:** I could take this to Bernice right now.

**Wendell Prewitt:** You could. You should, honestly, it is a good piece of work and she deserves the warning. And here is what I think happens, which I have thought about and I am not trying to be clever about it. She is bound by her promise. She cannot act on it before the sales mature without breaking it, and if she breaks it, Nebraska is gone and she knows it, and she will spend the next four years being right in public and watching the Union come apart. You can hand her this and you will have taken the only thing she owns.

*He stands up and takes his coat off the back of the chair.*

**Wendell Prewitt:** I am going to tell you what I would do if I were you, and then I am going to let you go because you have been in this room long enough to smell like a hotel. I would take the Illinois job. I would pay the fines, and there will be fines. And then when the harvest comes in badly, in a year or three, and every governor in that section turns to look at the one man who did not go under, I would want to be in the room when that happens. And you would have earned it, not stolen it.

**Player:** That is not the same thing.

**Wendell Prewitt:** No. It is better, and worse, and I have found that the two have never been in the same sentence anywhere in my life. Good day to you. It was a real pleasure, and I mean the pleasure part.

### Notes for implementation

- He is the antagonist that makes the economy a weapon. The player should be able to audit him with a Diplomacy or Streetcraft check, find the four Illinois buyers, and break him with a governor's commission rather than a single battle.
- His letters, ninety days ahead of every sale, are a great recurring artifact. Town rumor lines can quote them.
- Weakness to expose: he is not lying about anything, ever, which makes him the easiest antagonist to build a legal case against and the hardest to call a traitor.

## 4. MILTON CHAMBLISS

**Southern Compact rival. Leader of the Alignment in Tennessee, age 39. Holds Knoxville and a private army.**

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Traits | Valor 4, Mercy 1, Honor 3, Generosity 4, Calculation 3 |
| Skills | Leadership 5, Tactics 4, Close Combat 4, Streetcraft 4, Medicine 2 |
| Holds | Knoxville, six counties under a written compact, a training camp with a waiting list |
| Style | Sunday clothes on a Tuesday, a shotgun he carries like a cane, and a voice made for a room of two hundred |
| Ambition | Prove that he is the only man in the south who tells the truth, and that the south will believe him |
| Talks like | Preaching. He talks in parables about harvests and horses and he is not faking a single word of it |

### Backstory

Milton Chambliss was a county sheriff with a bad drinking problem and a real talent for standing up in a room, and he got sober at thirty one in a church basement in Morristown, which he will tell you about, and which is the entire reason he can do what he does.

He has filled a vacuum nobody else noticed. There are men all over Tennessee who own rifles and no organization, and the Compact was never going to organize them because Cordell Jessup runs on charm and there is a limit to how many charmed people you can put in a formation. Chambliss organized them anyway. He has a camp, a waiting list, a uniform standard that he insists is not a uniform, and a written compact with six counties that gives him roads and fuel in exchange for a garrison, and every line of it was negotiated by a lawyer and every signature is public.

He is Cordell Jessup without the talent for peace, and that is the thing nobody wants to say. He is not Jessup's enemy. Jessup has tried to co-opt him three times and each time Chambliss listened, agreed with him, understood the argument completely, and then did what he was going to do. He calls that being right late.

What people get wrong: he really does hang traitors. There have been four public hangings in six years, all of them men who sold movement information, all of them done after a trial that observers called fair and one of which was filmed. He is not cruel for pleasure. He has refused to hang two men his own officers begged him to hang. That restraint is what makes the other thing acceptable to him.

### What makes him dangerous

- He has the fastest, most motivated force on the map that does not answer to anybody, and the Southern Compact's signature mechanic is already fast mobilization. His version does not stop at ninety days, it has no ninety days, it just keeps coming.
- He wins the argument better than the leader does. Southern lords like him, and he is honest, and Generosity 4 means his men are the best fed on the map, which is the single best recruiting tool in the game.
- He is not trying to seize the Compact. He is trying to be so obviously more honest than Jessup that the Compact votes him in. That makes his whole campaign a legitimacy campaign, and a legitimacy campaign cannot be beaten by winning a battle.
- Mercy 1 and four hangings make him the only antagonist the player can execute or exile, and that choice should matter more here than anywhere else in the game.

- His four hangings should be a real quest chain with evidence, a lawyer, and a governor who will testify if handled correctly. That is the moral content this character exists to carry.
- Weakness to expose: he has never lied to anyone about anything checkable, which is the exact method that works on him.

## 5. SHERIDAN COBB

**Lone Star Frontier rival. Age 52. Home region: ranch country outside Abilene, now based in a courthouse he took by deed.**

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Traits | Valor 4, Mercy 3, Honor 2, Generosity 3, Calculation 4 |
| Skills | Leadership 4, Heavy Weapons 5, Athletics 5, Close Combat 4, Trade 3 |
| Holds | Three counties by deed and force, a private road with tolls, a hundred and forty riders |
| Style | A duster that fits, boots that do not, and a courthouse he keeps in good repair |
| Ambition | Own the only road between anywhere Texas matters, and be the only man nobody can go around |
| Talks like | Acres, fences, and weather. He answers a policy question with a cattle question and it is usually a good question |

### Backstory

Sheridan Cobb came up on a ranch that is not his, married into one that is, and got the deed to about nine thousand acres in a family dispute settled in a courthouse in about four minutes, which he has told the story of more than once. He did not steal it. That is the part that sticks. Everybody assumed he did and by the time they understood he had already built the fences.

He did not set out to own a county. He set out to fix a road. The road between three towns was bad, the county would not fix it, the state would not fix it, and so he graded it himself with his own equipment and his own men and put a toll on it, and the townspeople paid it gladly because it was the first decent road any of them had seen in a decade. Then somebody else built a road around his, so he bought the land on both sides of the detour, and that is how you end up with eleven miles of private right of way in the middle of a ranch country.

What makes him hard is that most of what he holds, people voted for. Three counties hold elections. He does not fix them. He has paid a school bond, he has paid a hospital, and when a county voted to move a road he paid for that too and did it the way they asked. Meanwhile every business that wants a loan, a truck, or a fence leans on him, and there is no fence in three counties that does not have his brand on the tape, and a man who sells the wire everybody fences with is not a governor, he is weather.

He has no claim to the Frontier and does not claim one. He calls the three counties his district and refers to Adair as the governor, and he returns a flag, and he puts his own line of men along the highway in clean pressed shirts, and he has never fired on a state patrolman in his life.

### What makes him dangerous

- He controls the road, which is the entire Frontier problem. Adair's advantage is speed, and speed is only worth anything if there is a road and somebody friendly at the end of it. Cobb decides both.
- He is the best mounted fighter on the map and he fights in terrain he has chosen, which is flat, open, and has one long approach road with nowhere for a cavalry raid to go. That is the High Ground mechanic turned sideways.
- He does not need to rebel. He has a legitimate local power base inside a section leader's territory that nobody has a lawful reason to touch, and he is always one cooperative gesture ahead of anybody who tries.
- He is Generosity 3 with Calculation 4, which means he will spend real money on loyalty and does it on schedule. Recruiting against him is not about ideology, it is about a better offer in writing.

### Threat monologue

*Delivered on his own front porch at the edge of the property, with a county deputy sitting in the truck and a visitor standing on the gravel, and the whole thing taking place with the fence in the background like a fence in a movie.*

**Sheridan Cobb:** Come on up, do not mind the gravel, I do not mind it. You have been down that road and you know exactly how long it took you and I have an idea of what you were counting.

**Player:** That is eleven minutes longer than the map says.

**Sheridan Cobb:** Eleven minutes is the whole subject of my life. Eleven minutes is a loaf of bread and a tank of gas and one man's temper. That is how often somebody in the panhandle gets to decide he is close enough to be angry.

**Sheridan Cobb:** Let me tell you what I do, because I would rather say it out loud. I do not want to be governor. I have been asked twice and I said no twice and I want that in the record. I want three counties to have a good road and a paid hospital and a school that is not leaking.

**Sheridan Cobb:** Now here is what the state of Texas does about that. Nothing. They have a highway department and about eleven thousand miles of road and they cannot put a culvert in the middle of your county without a hearing in somebody else's city, and by the time they have the hearing the dirt road has taken four trucks and one water tank off it this year and I know the names of all five men because their families are at my school.

**Sheridan Cobb:** So I did it. Me. My equipment, my men, my hours. And I did not do it for free, I put a toll on it, and I want you to sit with the fact that I put a toll on it, because I have thought about that more than the road.

**Sheridan Cobb:** A toll says this road costs money to keep, which is true, and the only people who ever complain are the people who did not want to pay anything for anything. I have had one preacher tell me the road ought to be free and I told him he could go build his own, and he has not brought it up since.

**Sheridan Cobb:** Here is what I am actually going to do, and it is not a threat, it is a schedule. In the spring I take my men and my equipment and I regrade the state highway from the county line, because I am tired of waiting for a hearing. In the summer the highway patrol comes out to find out whose road that is, and I will be extremely polite and I will have every deed in the truck with me, and we will stand in the sun for about an hour and everybody will go home.

**Sheridan Cobb:** In the fall the governor's office in Houston finds out that he has got a problem he cannot solve with a warrant, because I have not done a single thing that is against the law, which is a thing I have worked on for eleven years and I am extremely good at. And then Roy Adair and I sit down, and he is a decent man who has a real problem I can solve, and I bet him the toll money on the highway and the men and the road, and he pays, because his alternative is a two week march.

**Sheridan Cobb:** And that is it. That is the whole thing. No speech about liberty. Just a man on a road who got there first and knows what it costs to stay there. If the lord of Texas wants to buy me, he can buy me, and he should, because the alternative is a war and he has already told everybody he cannot afford two wars.

### Confrontation

*The player comes to the courthouse in the middle of the day and Cobb is on the steps with a man from the county extension office, showing him a map of a culvert, and he waves the player off and waits until the man is done, which takes four minutes and is itself a message.*

**Sheridan Cobb:** Sorry. He needs an answer about drainage and if I keep him here he goes around the horn. All right. You have been on my road, so you already know what you came to say, and I would rather get to the part that is interesting, so here it is.

**Sheridan Cobb:** Nope. Want to do this the fun way? Fine. I have got a warrant on me from a judge in Abilene who is my neighbor and I could get you a ride to the county line in about ten minutes, and I have got four minutes before that becomes a real option, so.

**Sheridan Cobb:** Now. *He turns the map around so the player is looking at it.*

**Sheridan Cobb:** There. That is the wash that goes under the state highway. It comes up about four feet in a storm and the culvert is undersized and the state has known since 2016. **Player:** You are proud of this.

**Sheridan Cobb:** I am fifty two years old and I have never once been ashamed of running a road. Put that in your report. The day I am ashamed of running it is the day somebody else runs it and we will both find out what that looks like.

### Notes for implementation

- His toll road should be a real mechanic on the map: a road that pays income to the player if they hold it, and a chokepoint that doubles Frontier march costs if he does.
- Three legitimate paths: the written deal, the insurance and testimony route that exposes his roads, or a fast cavalry fight at the long approach where his riders lose their advantage.
- Weakness to expose: he has built everything on being the person who already fixed the problem. A player who fixes one of the three counties problems before he does removes his entire argument.

## 6. LELAND SHAY

**Atlantic Corridor rival. Age 66. Holds four counties in Virginia under a private jurisdiction, and the only functioning court in the region.**

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Traits | Valor 2, Mercy 3, Honor 4, Generosity 2, Calculation 5 |
| Skills | Diplomacy 5, Engineering 3, Medicine 3, Trade 4, Streetcraft 4 |
| Holds | Four counties, a courthouse, a currency, and about four thousand people who would rather not be anywhere else |
| Style | Reading glasses on a chain, a cardigan, and a voice so reasonable it makes men confess things |
| Ambition | Prove that his four counties are better governed than the state they left, and be given a formal reason to keep doing it |
| Talks like | Conditions. He says if and then and provided that, and he is right so often it feels like cheating |

### Backstory

Leland Shay spent twenty two years in state administration in Virginia, most of it in a role nobody writes down, which is the one where you learn which laws are actually enforced and which are decoration. He knows the difference between the Comprehensive Emergency Management Act on paper and the four people in the county who will answer a phone at four in the morning.

When the structure came apart, Shay discovered that the counties inside the Corridor's own shadow had nobody. Not bad men, nobody. The old institutions were still answering mail three hundred miles north while the water came up in a town with no engineer, no judge, and no ordinance. He walked into a courthouse with a folding table and a generator and started issuing orders, and he was so obviously better at it that within a year four counties had quietly stopped sending anything to Richmond and started sending it to him.

He did not rebel. He resigned, properly, in writing, and he has never once referred to himself as anything but a former official of the Commonwealth, which is technically true and has become the most successful piece of propaganda in the eastern half of the country.

What people get wrong: he is the only antagonist in the game who is strictly better than the state he left. His counties have lower injury rates, faster court times, better school attendance, and no homelessness. Castille would have to invent a reason to move on him that is better than governing four counties well, and she has been trying for two years and cannot.

### What makes him dangerous

- He is the moral high ground, which in a game with a Why panel and a reputation system means every fight the player starts with him is uphill and every fight he starts with anybody else is easy.
- He has medicine and engineering and a working court, which is exactly the combination that makes a town survive the systems the game punishes hardest. He is immune to most of the failure states that kill other rulers.
- He has the one weapon nobody else on the map can use: legitimacy. Lords who would never accept Castille's creditors will take a contract from a man who wrote the emergency ordinance they are still using.
- He is patient past reason. He has spent two years doing nothing while the Corridor gets worse, because his counties get better while it gets worse, and that is a strategy.

### What makes him breakable

- He has never once been legally recognized, and that is a wound, not a pose. Give him the recognition and he becomes an official and stops being interesting.
- He cannot defend a claim to power without a claim to legitimacy, and legitimacy requires consent, and consent requires people to be able to leave. Every person who stays is also a person who has not chosen him.

### Threat monologue

*Delivered on the courthouse steps in front of nobody, at four in the afternoon, because he does not do public things. A visitor is walking up the walk and he waits for them to be in earshot, then starts talking, which is a thing he planned.*

**Leland Shay:** You are welcome to sit. There is no reason to hurry, I have nothing until Thursday and I understand that is a luxury sentence in your line of work.

**Player:** People say you seceded.

**Leland Shay:** People say a great many things and most of them are aimed at me, which is at least a compliment. No, I resigned. I resigned in writing and my letter was three pages and it thanked everyone by name, and I have never since referred to myself in print as anything but a former official of the Commonwealth.

**Leland Shay:** But let us set aside the vocabulary, since that is not what you came for. Ask me instead what happened the week the river came up in a town of four thousand people ninety miles from the nearest hospital that was still open. Four things happened, and I will tell you all four.

**Leland Shay:** One. Somebody answered the phone, because I had a list of sixty names on it and it had been a list for three years before anything broke. Two. I opened a courthouse on a generator I bought with my own money and I did not have to invent a single legal authority, because I had read the enabling statute in 2018 and I knew that the governor's authority delegated to counties on exactly this question. Three. We opened a school gym and moved forty people upstairs and put a generator in the corridor, and we wrote the order ourselves because nobody from the capital would write it for us.

**Leland Shay:** And four. We were, by the middle of the second week, the part of Virginia that worked. Not the part that was best served. The part that worked.

**Player:** So you took the county.

**Leland Shay:** The county stopped sending us to a capital that could not answer a phone. I want to be precise, because precision is the only thing I have. Nobody took anything. ### Notes for implementation

- The full win condition should include a legal path (compact with a signature line) and a cruel path (take the counties and watch the numbers fall), and both should be available with no warning from the game.
- Weakness to expose: he cannot want anything, which reads as principle and is actually absence of appetite. Offer him the thing he claims not to want and watch what happens.

## 7. WHAT ALL SIX HAVE IN COMMON

The player meets these six the way they meet the faction leaders, but the conversation goes differently, and the structural rules are:

1. **Every antagonist is inside somebody else's territory.** Five of six are rivals to a playable section leader, which means the player has to pick a side first and then work against its leader's judgment. That tension is the point. It should be uncomfortable and legible.
2. **None of them can be defeated by the obvious method.** No one here is beaten by charging them with more men. Four are beaten by supply, two by legitimacy, one by paperwork, one by a better offer in writing. The army is the default and the default should be the wrong answer.
3. **Every one of them can be joined.** If the player backs the Alignment, buys the toll road, takes Prewitt's Illinois job, or signs Shay's compact, the antagonist becomes a ruler in the game and the section leader has to live with it. That is a valid campaign.
4. **Every one is wrong about one thing.** Prewitt thinks honesty immunizes him. Chambliss thinks he is the only one who can be trusted. Ashby thinks roads are the only real power. Alcaraz thinks people will pay for being reasonable. Cobb thinks being early is the same as being right. Shay thinks good numbers are an argument. These are the player's levers.
5. **The generous ones are not the safe ones and the merciful ones are not the weak ones.** Alcaraz is Mercy 1 and has killed three communities without firing a shot. Cobb is Mercy 3 and will fight you in his own front yard. Do not let the trait numbers predict the threat level; the threats are structural.
6. **Their tools are visible before their armies are.** A pumping station, a concrete pad, a filed letter, a courthouse porch, a toll gate, a sheet of paper with a number on it. If the player gets good intelligence early, from Vega or from a captured clerk, the whole fight is half over. Reward scouting with real content.
