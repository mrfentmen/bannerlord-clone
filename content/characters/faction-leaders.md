# FACTION LEADERS

The six side leaders, one per section from FACTIONS.md section 4. They are the people the player swears to, works for, betrays, replaces, or kills.

Design rules these follow:

- Real geography, fictional people. No leader is a real person, no leader's history replays a real war, attack, disaster, or crisis. Per CONSTITUTION.md section 6.
- Every leader is playable as a starting side choice, so each one is written to be something the player wants for a while and then regrets.
- Each leader's personality is built from their faction's actual problem. A Pacific Compact leader worries about food. A Mountain Alliance leader worries about authority. The trait is the problem, given a body.
- Traits and skills use the vocabulary from RULERS.md sections 3 and 4, so a leader can be scored by the same code that scores a generated lord.
- Dialogue is plain spoken American English. Contractions, sentence fragments, and the occasional regional rhythm. No one speaks in a briefing document.

## 1. MARISOL VEGA

**Pacific Compact leader. Age 46. Home region: California's Central Valley, now based in Los Angeles.**

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Traits | Valor 2, Mercy 3, Honor 4, Generosity 2, Calculation 5 |
| Skills | Logistics 5, Intelligence 5, Diplomacy 4, Medicine 3, Tactics 3 |
| Holds | Los Angeles, San Diego, Oakland, Long Beach terminal |
| Style | Marinated denim, steel-toed boots she never wears, a tablet she does not put down |
| Ambition | Keep the Compact solvent through a bad harvest without closing a single port |
| Talks like | Supply chains. She measures everything in days of supply and will tell you what you are worth in trucks |

### Backstory

Marisol Vega grew up in a house outside Fresno where the ceiling leaked into a bucket and her mother ran the numbers at the kitchen table while their landlord talked about the harvest like a favor he was doing them. There was never enough food. There was always work. She learned early that most disasters in this country are not explosions, they are a spreadsheet that ran out of margin.

She drove a forklift at fourteen, then a box truck, then a fleet of them. By thirty-four she was running logistics for one of the two ports nobody ever photographs, the ugly one full of grain terminals and container yards, and she was very good at it. She knows how many trucks it takes to move a week's worth of food into a city of four million and she knows what happens on day eight.

When the federal institutions came apart, she was the one who kept the Los Angeles basin fed for nine days by talking a convoy through a checkpoint with nothing but a clipboard and a spreadsheet of who was hungry where. She did not plan a revolution. She just noticed that a map of need was a kind of authority, and she was holding one.

She took the Compact because nobody else wanted it. The coastal cities thought inland counties were subsidized. The inland counties thought the coast took their water and gave them a lecture. Marisol is the only person in California who is actually angry at both of them, and that turned out to be a unifying force.

What she does not say in public: she has run out of trucks before, and people died of it, and she has decided that this will not happen on her watch. What she does not say in private either. It is the whole engine of her personality.

### First meeting

*Your party arrives at the Long Beach terminal office at seven in the morning. She is standing at a whiteboard covered in colored tape, arguing with two port captains. She holds up one finger to you without turning around, which is how you know she knows you are there.*

**Marisol Vega:** You are the reason I am thirty minutes behind. Come in. Close the door, not all the way, I like knowing where my exits are.

**Player:** My lord. You honor me.

**Marisol Vega:** Do not do that. I have four port captains and a governor who all want to "honor" me and I am so tired of being flattered by men who want something.

**Player:** Then let me be direct. I am here to offer my company to the Compact.

**Marisol Vega:** To the Compact, or to the shipping lanes? Because those are different customers and one of them pays on time.

*She finally turns around. She looks tired in a way that is not recent.*

**Marisol Vega:** Sit down. Here is my situation and I am going to give it to you the way I give it to everybody, which is badly and without decoration. California has eleven days of grain. Not eleven days of food, eleven days of grain, because we have protein and we have nuts and we have the fruit coming, but the grain is the spine. Everything else bends around it.

**Player:** Eleven days is not a crisis.

**Marisol Vega:** Eleven days is a crisis on day nine. On day nine a city is not short of wheat, a city is short of confidence, and confidence is what makes a city let its trucks through a roadblock. I have watched that movie. I do not want to be in it.

**Player:** What do you need from me?

**Marisol Vega:** Volume. Not heroics. I do not need you to be brave, I need you to be somewhere on a schedule, repeatedly, with men who can carry.

*She slides a tablet across the table. It is a map of the eastern counties and there are red pins on it.*

**Marisol Vega:** Inland is about to start fighting me. I have got the coastal cities thinking they are the only ones with a food problem, which is not true but which is useful to them, and I have got the valley wanting me to allocate by how many people they have rather than by how many trucks I have. Both of them are right and I only have enough of one of them.

**Player:** So you need a soldier who can sit at a table with two people who both think they are being robbed.

**Marisol Vega:** Now you understand the job. Nobody else in this building understands the job.

*She picks up the tablet again. Something on the map has clearly gone wrong today.*

**Marisol Vega:** Tell me what you have actually got. Men, horses, trucks, and I mean actually got, not the number you would say in front of a lord.

**Player:** Fifty men. Twelve horses. Two trucks that I am fond of and one that I am not.

**Marisol Vega:** Fond of. God help us, we are all going to be talking like that.

**Player:** Do you have an offer for me?

**Marisol Vega:** I have three. The first is honest and boring: a contract, paid weekly, escort work on the inland runs. The second is mine and it is not a contract: if you go out there and hold one road for one month, I will remember it when the governors start arguing about who inherits this thing after I am gone. The third is bad and you should know about it.

**Player:** Go on.

**Marisol Vega:** Somewhere in the next year this breaks, one of the counties declares for the Mountain Alliance, and I will need men who will hold a road against people who live in the mountains. That is real and it is coming. I am not going to lie to you about it and then be surprised later.

**Player:** I will take the second.

**Marisol Vega:** Of course you will. Everybody takes the second and then complains about the first four months, which is fine, that is what it is for. Do not ask me for money first, ask me in a week.

*She hands you back the tablet, then stops, then adds one more thing.*

**Marisol Vega:** One more thing, and then you can go. If you ever see a child on the road with a shopping cart, you stop. You do not take the cart. You stop. I have had this rule for eleven years and it has never once cost me anything.

**Player:** Why?

**Marisol Vega:** Because a person who will not stop for a shopping cart is not going to stop for a roadblock either, and I need my people stopping for roadblocks.

### Notes for implementation

- Her first-meeting request is a **quest offer** (hold the inland road for a month) that pays in loyalty rather than gold, which is the Pacific way of buying loyalty.
- Her trait line is deliberately Calculation 5 with Mercy 3. She is not cruel, she is just always doing arithmetic while you are deciding.
- Weakness to expose in play: she underestimates cavalry and generosity. Give her a lord who is warm and reckless and watch her learn to need him.

## 2. HOLLIS GRANT

**Mountain Alliance leader. Age 63. Home region: Butte, Montana, now based in Denver.**

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Traits | Valor 4, Mercy 4, Honor 5, Generosity 3, Calculation 2 |
| Skills | Engineering 5, Tactics 4, Logistics 3, Medicine 2, Diplomacy 2 |
| Holds | Denver, Salt Lake City, Albuquerque |
| Style | Same wool coat since the eighties, a hard hat he wears out of habit not fashion |
| Ambition | Keep the Alliance whole by making sure nobody important feels like a subject |
| Talks like | Slow and literal. He respects people who finish a sentence and distrusts people who do not |

### Backstory

Hollis Grant came out of the Butte mines at twenty-one, which in his telling was the day the mountain gave him a job and he has been working for it ever since. He was underground for nine years. He knows the shape of the tunnels under four states better than he knows the shape of his own house, and he has pulled forty-one men out of holes that were supposed to be surveyed.

That is why he is the one holding the Alliance together. The section has no authority in it. Rulers in Salt Lake and Phoenix and Reno do not take orders from Denver and never will, and Hollis did not fix that by asserting more authority. He fixed it by being the man who shows up when a mine roof fails, in weather that kills, with money that he raises himself and gives away. A man who can make a road appear in a crisis does not have to explain why anyone should follow him the rest of the time.

He is not a general in the way the other five leaders are generals. He has fought three real engagements, all defensive, all in passes he chose because he knew the ground. Two of them he won by making the attackers walk for two days through country they did not have to walk through, which is the oldest trick in the book and works every single time because nobody wants to believe their enemy is smart.

What people underestimate: he is not patient about politics, he is patient about everything else. He will wait out a siege for months. He cannot wait out a bad mood in a tavern for one evening.

What he is bad at: he cannot project force past his own mountains. He knows this. He has written three memoranda about it and been ignored three times by men who own tanks.

### First meeting

*The meeting is in a warehouse outside Denver that used to hold something heavy. Hollis is behind a steel table with a paper map on it, holding a coffee cup he has not drunk from. Two of his own governors are already in the room and they are arguing about a grain shipment, which stops when he comes in, and starts again almost immediately when he sits down.*

**Hollis Grant:** Sit down. No, that chair is broken. That one.

**Player:** My lord.

**Hollis Grant:** Grant. I have been called Governor, Chairman, and Your Excellency by men I have never once helped, and every one of them was being polite. Grant is fine. Are you the one they told me about?

**Player:** I am. I held the Four Corners crossing for the Alliance last season.

**Hollis Grant:** Three weeks.

**Player:** Five weeks.

**Hollis Grant:** Three weeks, according to my man who was there. Then you left with the road open and took the south wall with you, which was the smart part. Nobody talks about the smart part. Sit down, I want to ask you something and you will not like it.

**Player:** Ask.

**Hollis Grant:** Who in your own command did you trust to hold your back while you held the front?

*The two governors stop arguing and listen. This is the interview.*

**Player:** A sergeant named Ida Wells. She held the north wall with nineteen people and no complaint.

**Hollis Grant:** Nineteen.

**Player:** Nineteen.

**Hollis Grant:** See, I asked that for a reason and I want you to notice the reason. You just told me the truth in front of two men who were about to talk about you behind your back. That is worth more to me than the crossing. You do not know that yet, you will in a year.

**Player:** What do you need?

**Hollis Grant:** I need to be honest with you, which I am not often, so pay attention.

*The coffee cup still has not been touched.*

**Hollis Grant:** This Alliance works because nobody in it is truly in charge. I do not command Salt Lake City. I do not command Phoenix, where it has been ninety-four degrees and they have been furious since May. I do not command Albuquerque and I have stopped trying. If you look at the map you will see six states that have agreed to share a road system and a mining code and nothing else.

**Player:** And that is your strength.

**Hollis Grant:** That is my luck that has not run out yet. It works right up until it does not, and when it stops working it will stop all at once and not in the middle, which is the part that keeps me up.

**Player:** So what do you want from me?

**Hollis Grant:** I want you to hold something in the east. Flat ground, no passes, no high ground, nothing in your favor. The Corridor has money and they are putting it into men who can stand on a plain and shoot, and I cannot stop that from Montana. You can.

**Player:** Flat ground is not my kind of ground.

**Hollis Grant:** I know. I am asking anyway. Here is why, and I am not going to dress it up.

**Hollis Grant:** We are the smallest section by people and the richest by metal, and that means every army on this map that wants a serious war with us has to come to us, and I can make them lose men doing it. But if they ever decide not to come to us, if they ever just go around, then this whole argument about how hard we are to invade becomes a thing men tell each other. I would rather have one honest fighter on the flat than four more people in Denver telling me we are safe.

**Player:** That is a very honest answer, my lord.

**Hollis Grant:** It is a practical answer. You are taking it as an honest one because you are a decent man, which is fine, but do not make a habit of it, decent men get used.

*He finally puts the coffee down, apparently deciding it was not going to be finished.*

**Hollis Grant:** One thing before you go. If the Corridor sends you a peace offering, take it. Read it twice and take it. I am old enough to know that a fight you can win in the mountains is a fight you can also avoid, and every year I put men on that ridgeline for things that could have been a letter.

**Player:** Does it bother you that you can win at home?

**Hollis Grant:** It bothers me constantly. Sit down for that one.

**Player:** I am already sitting.

**Hollis Grant:** Good. Then you know how it feels.

### Notes for implementation

- His quest chain is defensive and slow, with the payoff always being that a battle never happened. That is his whole thesis and it should be playable.
- Calculation 2 is the vulnerability. A clever, manipulative lord can outmaneuver him politically and the player can watch it happen.
- The nineteen on the north wall is a quest seed. There is a real character called Ida Wells in the design and she is not a real person.

## 3. BERNICE OYELARAN

**Great Lakes Union leader. Age 51. Home region: Cedar County, Iowa, now based in Chicago.**

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Traits | Valor 3, Mercy 4, Honor 4, Generosity 5, Calculation 3 |
| Skills | Diplomacy 5, Logistics 4, Leadership 4, Medicine 2, Tactics 2 |
| Holds | Chicago, Des Moines, Omaha, Cedar County farm |
| Style | Work boots, a coat from a farm supply store, a wedding band she never took off |
| Ambition | Feed nine states without becoming the reason one of them starves |
| Talks like | Farmers. Weather, soil, prices, and the year. She compares everything to a harvest and she is usually right |

### Backstory

Bernice Oyelaran grew up on four hundred acres of corn and soybeans that her family did not own, worked for a share, and nearly lost twice. In 1998 the price did what prices do and the share came in below what the inputs had cost, and her father drove to Des Moines to sell the combine, and she rode along. She has never fully gotten over that truck ride. It is the reason she counts every grain elevator in her section the way other leaders count their soldiers.

She did not go into politics because she loves it. She went into politics because the negotiating table for grain was in a room she was already in, as the person whose job was to know the numbers. She learned that the Corridor could fund a war with money from eight states and could not feed a city of one million for ten days, and that this made it the most dangerous thing in America, and that she was the only person at the table who understood both halves of that sentence.

She built the Lakes Union on one simple promise to every governor in it: I will never sell your grain out from under you without telling you first. She has kept it for six years at enormous cost. It is why Nebraska hates her. Nebraska has wanted an open market for a century and Bernice has held the gate shut on it out of principle, and Nebraska has a very good case.

What people get wrong about her: she is not a peacemaker. She has fought three governors who broke that promise and she broke them back. The difference between her and the Southern leader is not gentleness, it is that Bernice picks her fights and announces them.

### First meeting

*The meeting is in Chicago, in a room booked under a name that is not hers, in a building on the south side with no lobby and a working elevator. She is early. She has brought you coffee and has already started eating a sandwich, which tells you more about her mood than her face does.*

**Bernice Oyelaran:** Sit, sit, that chair is fine, everything in this room is fine, the coffee is from a place I like and I drove past it this morning. How do you like the drive in?

**Player:** Long, my lord.

**Bernice Oyelaran:** You came from Cedar County. You did not come from Cedar County, you came from somewhere near it with the same flat nothing and the same sky. Was it Iowa?

**Player:** Iowa.

**Bernice Oyelaran:** Then you already know what I am going to say, which saves us time, so I will say it fast and then you can tell me what is wrong with it. The Lakes Union has twelve million people, more than any section, and it eats more than it grows by about forty percent. Every single day. That is not a problem, that is just the shape of the place. The problem is that five other sections have noticed.

**Player:** They have noticed.

**Bernice Oyelaran:** They have noticed and they have started being very polite about it, and I am telling you that being polite is how they do it, because a man who is going to take your grain does not raid your grain. He sends a delegation. He sends a delegation with a very good argument about shared prosperity, and by the time you have heard the whole argument you have already promised him eleven percent.

*She wipes her hands on a napkin. It is a habit she has never outgrown.*

**Bernice Oyelaran:** Here is my job, and it is a stupid job, and nobody else wants it. I have to sell grain and I have to keep grain, at the same time, forever, with no gap. That is it. That is the entire leadership of nine states.

**Player:** That sounds impossible.

**Bernice Oyelaran:** It is impossible. It is also the only thing that keeps Nebraska from leaving, so it is impossible and mandatory. What I have to say to you next is a selling job, and I am going to be upfront that it is a selling job.

**Player:** Go ahead.

**Bernice Oyelaran:** The Southern Compact mobilizes faster than anyone. And they are right to. They have four hundred thousand people who own a rifle and have been told all their lives that the smart thing is to keep one in the truck. I am not going to insult you by pretending that is not a real army. If they come, my men will hold a line, and I will feed them, because I can feed nine states for eight months if I have to and I have run the numbers so many times they are worn smooth.

**Player:** And what do you want?

**Bernice Oyelaran:** I want a man who can stand in a field of corn in October and be believed. That is the actual request and I am aware of how stupid it sounds. Our soldiers are farmworkers and mechanics and schoolteachers. They are the best motivated people in this country and the least impressive-looking, and every propaganda war in the world is won by whoever has the better uniforms.

**Player:** So you need me to look frightening.

**Bernice Oyelaran:** I need you to look like somebody who has been there. That is different and it is better.

**Player:** What is in it for me?

*She reaches into a paper bag on the table. She puts it on the table and pushes it toward you. It is a sack of corn, which is ridiculous, and she knows it is ridiculous.*

**Bernice Oyelaran:** That is from my own farm. Not from the Union, not from a stockpile, from four hundred acres my family worked and does not own. People find that sentimental. It is not sentiment, it is a receipt. I am showing you I am not talking about the abstract good of the Union, I am talking about a specific field that I put my name on.

**Player:** Where does the rest of the money come from?

**Bernice Oyelaran:** There is no rest of the money. If you join me you get grain, meat, a place to sleep, and a share of what the land produces, which is a great deal more than it sounds like when you have never farmed. I am not the Atlantic Corridor. I cannot buy your loyalty with a check and I have stopped trying to pretend otherwise.

**Player:** My lord, that is a very honest offer.

**Bernice Oyelaran:** It is a very fair offer, and those are not the same, but they are close enough that I will take it. Now. Let me ask you the hard one, because if I do not ask you now somebody else will ask you later and it will be at a worse moment.

**Player:** Go ahead.

**Bernice Oyelaran:** Nebraska wants my grain markets open. Every year. And every year I say no and every year the argument gets a little uglier, and this year I am running out of reasons that are not just me being stubborn. If somebody offers you a better deal somewhere else, I do not want to find out about it from a courier. I want you to bring it to me. Even if it is tempting. Especially if it is tempting.

*She stands up, which is the signal that this part of the meeting is done, and sits back down again, which is the signal that it is not.*

**Bernice Oyelaran:** Actually, one more thing, and my daughter told me to say it. She said, tell him about the thing where you bought the wrong tractor.

**Player:** My lord?

**Bernice Oyelaran:** In ninety-nine I approved a combine. It was wrong. It cost the farm sixty thousand dollars and I defended the decision for a week before a mechanic named Ray told me I had bought a machine for a farm with no hill in it, and there was no hill in it. I was right about the money and wrong about the field. I have thought about that a lot since, and most of what I have learned about leading people fits in that sentence.

**Player:** What is the lesson, my lord?

**Bernice Oyelaran:** Know what your field looks like before you buy the machine.

### Notes for implementation

- Generosity 5 with Calculation 3. She is the most genuinely good leader in the game and also the one most likely to be quietly destroyed by a debt.
- Her quest line is the hardest to run: the grain dilemma. Selling is a real reward, hoarding is a real reward, and both should feel correct.
- The combine is a recurring callback. It can appear in a late conversation as a rare, high-value trust moment.

## 4. CORDELL JESSUP

**Southern Compact leader. Age 44. Home region: outside Memphis, now based in Atlanta.**

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Traits | Valor 5, Mercy 2, Honor 2, Generosity 4, Calculation 3 |
| Skills | Leadership 5, Tactics 4, Diplomacy 4, Medicine 3, Logistics 2 |
| Holds | Atlanta, Nashville, New Orleans, Jacksonville |
| Style | Tailored but never buttoned, a hat he takes off indoors and never puts on outdoors |
| Ambition | Keep ten quarrelsome lords following him for exactly as long as it takes to win |
| Talks like | Fast. Warm. Calls everyone friend, which means he likes you about forty percent of the time |

### Backstory

Cordell Jessup was a military police sergeant in a real army that no longer exists in the same form, and the job made him very good at two things. He could tell in about four seconds whether a room of angry men was about to break, and he could make it not break, usually by being the most relaxed person in it.

He was not political until the institutions came apart. He was running a training camp for county sheriffs who had more rifles than training manuals, and the Compact formed around him because he had the one thing nobody else in the south had, which was a room where all the competing lords had been in the same building and nobody had been killed.

He holds the Compact together by making every one of its ten states believe they have a private relationship with him. Governor Dumas in Louisiana believes they are friends. The Alignment in Tennessee believes they are friends. They are not friends, and he has never lied to them about anything that could be checked, which is the only reason it works. His reputation for honesty is a tool he maintains on purpose.

What people get wrong: the charisma is real. He is not performing it. He genuinely likes people, genuinely enjoys the work, and genuinely cannot tell the difference between a person he likes and a person who is useful to him, which is exactly the flaw that eventually ruins men like him.

What he does not admit to anybody, including his own staff: he has never in his life started a fight he did not have a plan to end.

### First meeting

*The meeting is on a porch in Atlanta that is somehow still the most important room in the southern states. There is a game on the table that neither of you are playing. Cordell comes out to the rail, shakes your hand, and holds it a half second longer than a stranger requires, which is the whole method in one gesture.*

**Cordell Jessup:** Friend. You are shorter than the reports said, and I mean that as a compliment, because the reports said you were enormous and reports are usually wrong.

**Player:** My lord.

**Cordell Jessup:** No, no. Jessup. Cordell. The word lord is for the pamphlets. Sit down, the chair's fine, I promise you the chair is fine, we get forty people on this porch when we need to.

**Player:** You summoned me.

**Cordell Jessup:** I did. I will be honest with you, because you have a reputation for being honest back and I have a short amount of time. I am in trouble that is not a military trouble. That is important to say first. Everybody comes down here expecting to be let into a war council and what I actually need is somebody to sit in a room with two men who hate each other and keep them in the room.

**Player:** Which two men?

**Cordell Jessup:** Well now you are asking the right question, which is why I called you.

*He leans on the rail. The good cheer drops about twenty percent, which for him is a complete change of demeanor.*

**Cordell Jessup:** Governor Dumas out of Louisiana. Colonel Boatwright, who runs the Alignment in Tennessee and is not actually a colonel and never has been, but will die if you call him that. They have hated each other for two years and it started over a pontoon bridge and it has stopped being about a pontoon bridge.

**Player:** What do they want now?

**Cordell Jessup:** Everything, both of them, and nothing, neither of them. Boatwright wants Memphis and the river yards, Dumas wants the Deep South and a seat at the table without the Compact. And if those two walk out of that room, this section does not have a leader, it has two halves that shoot at each other over Tennessee, and I cannot fight that war, I have tried, I lost, it took four months and I do not have four months.

**Player:** So you want me to be in the room.

**Cordell Jessup:** I want you to be in the room. And I want to be very clear that I am not asking you to solve it, because you cannot and I cannot. I am asking you to make them both feel like they won something, which is a thing you can do and it is a thing almost nobody can do.

**Player:** And if I cannot?

**Cordell Jessup:** Then you will be the man who tried, and I will remember that better than I will remember success, because I have a lot of success and not enough tries.

*He straightens up and the warmth comes back, like a light switched on.*

**Cordell Jessup:** Now. The other half of this, because you did not come three hundred miles to be a chaperone. We are the fastest mobilization on this map and I am not exaggerating. Ten states, and when I call, men come. Days, not weeks. Volunteers, mostly, and do not tell me about the romance of the volunteer, because I know exactly how fast a volunteer army goes home, I have watched it happen twice.

**Player:** Then what is the advantage?

**Cordell Jessup:** The advantage is the first ninety days. Nobody else in this country can put two hundred thousand men in a field in ninety days. We win in the first ninety days or we lose slowly, and slowly is worse than losing, I would take the loss.

**Player:** And after the ninety days?

**Cordell Jessup:** After the ninety days I have fewer cards than everybody, which is the deal I have been given, and I play it anyway.

**Player:** What are you offering me?

**Cordell Jessup:** Whatever you want. That is not a trick, that is my actual position. Money, land, a command, a town, a very nice house in a warm climate. Whatever. I have a lot of men and I do not have a lot of good reasons to be loyal to me.

**Player:** Why not?

**Cordell Jessup:** Because I have spent six years being the guy who is fun to be around, and I have got to the point where I can feel it when a room goes cold on me, and it goes cold on me more than it used to.

*He laughs at himself, which is genuine, and then the rail and the smile are gone again for a second.*

**Cordell Jessup:** Here is the thing I have never said out loud. If I can hold the Compact together for six more years, the states inside it will have built their own supply lines, their own officers, their own reasons to be a section instead of ten separate arguments. Then it does not need me. I am not trying to be necessary. I am trying to be unnecessary in a way that survives me.

**Player:** You should tell people that.

**Cordell Jessup:** Do not do that. If I say that out loud, Dumas hears it and thinks I am preparing to retire, and Boatwright hears it and thinks he has a window.

**Player:** Understood.

**Cordell Jessup:** Good man. One more question and then I will let you go, because it is late and I have to go be charming at somebody else.

**Player:** Ask.

**Cordell Jessup:** If you were me, and you had to leave the Compact in a year, what would you do first?

*He watches you answer. Whatever you say, he does not react to it at all, which is how you know he is listening.*

### Notes for implementation

- His loyalty system is the inverse of everyone else's. Governors defect not from neglect but from being kept at arm's length too long.
- Valor 5 and Mercy 2: he will take a city that would cost fewer lives if starved, and he will not enjoy it afterward.
- The room between Dumas and Boatwright is a repeatable quest hub, and it can be failed in ways that permanently split the section.

## 5. ROYCE ADAIR

**Lone Star Frontier leader. Age 49. Home region: ranch country outside San Angelo, now based in Houston.**

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Traits | Valor 3, Mercy 2, Honor 3, Generosity 3, Calculation 4 |
| Skills | Logistics 4, Leadership 4, Trade 5, Driving 4, Tactics 3 |
| Holds | Houston, Dallas, San Antonio, the ranch |
| Style | A hat, a truck that has been in the dirt, boots he does not clean |
| Ambition | Hold the greatest stretch of land on the map without becoming the shortest war in it |
| Talks like | Blunt, transactional, impatient with ceremony. Will state his price before you ask for anything |

### Backstory

Royce Adair has been a rich man in Texas for all of his life and has never once pretended otherwise, which is the reason people either trust him immediately or never. His grandfather found oil under pasture that had been cattle since the 1840s, and the family has spent three generations converting animals into a currency nobody in the house was ever taught to be careful with.

He grew up on the ranch, not in Houston. He still spends three weeks a year moving cattle with men whose entire resumes consist of being good at this. He can ride better than anyone in the Compact and it is not close, and he knows exactly how little that is worth against an enemy who does not have to close, and it frustrates him constantly.

He took the Frontier because nobody else wanted a section that is mostly empty. The problem he inherited is arithmetic: the largest territory by area on the map, borders four rivals, no natural allies, and every march a hundred miles inland costs more food and money than the same march done by somebody standing still. His entire strategy has been to refuse to fight more than one war at a time and to move faster than anybody who is not also moving.

What people get wrong: they assume he is simple because he is direct. He is the only one of the six who has run his own supply chain and therefore the only one who has a real answer when asked how long a war can be sustained. His answer is always the same and it is never good enough.

### First meeting

*The meeting is outside San Antonio, not Houston, which tells you something. Royce is standing next to a truck at a gate with a rifle leaning against the fender, not holding it. He does not shake your hand until you have been standing there for a second, which is also a test.*

**Royce Adair:** You are the man from the Corridor job.

**Player:** I am.

**Royce Adair:** Two hundred miles of dirt road through country I do not control, and you did it in nine days with sixty men and forty of them on foot. My people said it would take sixteen.

**Player:** Your people were right about the road.

**Royce Adair:** Yeah, well, my people have never run supply through that road either. You want to know what I think happened? You did not do it faster than the road allows. You did it faster than your own men expected, and men who are beat but not surprised keep walking.

**Player:** That is a generous reading, my lord.

**Royce Adair:** It is an accurate one. Stop calling me that, I have never once been called that by anybody who fought anything. Adair.

*He picks up the rifle, checks that the chamber is clear out of pure habit, and leans it back.*

**Royce Adair:** Straight to it, because I owe you about four days of your time and I am not going to waste it being polite. Here is my position, here is my problem, here is what I pay, and then you can tell me no, which is fine and expected.

**Player:** Please.

**Royce Adair:** Texas and Oklahoma. Nine hours of road between any two of anything. Two big rivers with bad bridges and worse ferries. Three cities worth more than the rest of the section combined, spread across a space the size of Germany, and I cannot get reinforcements to any of them in under two weeks.

**Player:** That is a difficult map.

**Royce Adair:** That is a bill. Everything about the Frontier is a bill that comes due later. Ask me what my army looks like and I will tell you it is smaller than everybody's and better than everybody's, and both of those are true, and neither one is the problem.

**Player:** Then what is the problem?

**Royce Adair:** Distance. Every day my horse is away from the ranch it costs me food it should be selling and money it should be making. If I send five hundred men from San Antonio to El Paso, four hundred of them are riding, eating, and not fighting, for nine days. If somebody hits my back while they are out there, I lose. That is not a tactic, that is just the shape of the ground.

*He looks at the gate, then back at you.*

**Royce Adair:** So here is what I actually want, and it is not an army. Everybody offers me an army, because an army is the only thing that looks like a solution. What I want is a man who can be in Abilene on a Tuesday and in Amarillo on a Saturday and be useful in both places without being tired, which means somebody who thinks about distance the way other people think about walls.

**Player:** What would that pay?

**Royce Adair:** Ranch land, if you want it, and I will not sell it to you, I will give it to you, which is a different arrangement and I have never offered it to four people in my life. Twenty head to start with, a house with a well, and a horse. I am also going to pay you a percentage of anything you bring in that I did not have before, and I want to be clear that I mean sales, not conquest, though I would not complain about conquest.

**Player:** That is generous.

**Royce Adair:** It is a deposit. I am buying you, and I would rather do it in front of you where you know exactly what you were bought with than pretend we are friends and settle up later when you are angry about it.

*He grins at that, and it is the first thing he has done that is not information delivery.*

**Royce Adair:** Now the part I would ask you not to repeat. Three years ago the Corridor offered me a loan. Very good terms, very friendly people, and I have never met anyone from there who uses the word friendly the way those people use it. They did not ask me for anything. That is what I want you to understand about the Atlantic Corridor. They never ask. They just arrange the future so that you owe them and then they wait.

**Player:** You turned them down.

**Royce Adair:** I refinanced them twice and turned them down twice. That is the entire answer and it is very boring. Do not do what I did unless you have a ranch, I only had a ranch because of luck.

**Player:** What is the hardest part of holding this?

**Royce Adair:** People think it is the enemies. It is that I have four borders and every single one of them wants to be my friend right now, and being my friend means I owe somebody something, and I do not have the food to owe anybody with. Every offer I have had in six years has been a way of putting somebody's flag on my land and calling it partnership.

**Player:** And you said no.

**Royce Adair:** I said no six times. Say no seven.

*He holds out his hand this time, properly, and does not rush it.*

**Royce Adair:** One last thing. My section is called the Lone Star Frontier and everybody thinks that is romantic. It is not romantic, it is a warning. I have more room than anyone and I have run out of people to fill it more times than I have won a fight. If you want a place where your work gets seen, come here. If you want a place where you will be bored and stretched thin for nine years, come here too, but know that is what you are buying.

**Player:** Which is it?

**Royce Adair:** Both. It is always both out here.

### Notes for implementation

- Trade 5 makes him the natural partner for the player's merchant and caravan skills, and the natural source of the game's best horses.
- His difficulty is that he offers land, not titles. A player who wants to be a lord will chafe at a section run on deals.
- The Corridor loan is a recurring event that pays out well every time and slowly strangles the player.

## 6. YVONNE CASTILLE

**Atlantic Corridor leader. Age 57. Home region: born in Baltimore, raised in Fairfax, now based in Washington and New York.**

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Traits | Valor 1, Mercy 2, Honor 3, Generosity 1, Calculation 5 |
| Skills | Diplomacy 5, Trade 5, Intelligence 4, Medicine 2, Tactics 3 |
| Holds | New York, Philadelphia, Boston, Washington, Baltimore |
| Style | Plain dark clothes, no jewelry, a paper cup she refills herself from a carafe |
| Ambition | Keep the Corridor solvent and unlovable, which is the only shape that survives |
| Talks like | Precise and conditional. She says "if" more than she says "when". She has never raised her voice and has never needed to |

### Backstory

Yvonne Castille spent twenty-two years in federal administration, which in practical terms means she spent twenty-two years learning exactly which promises the government could not keep. She was a mid-level official in a mid-level agency handling a budget nobody would discuss, and when the structure came apart she found that the people left holding it were mostly administrators, because administrators are the ones who know where the money goes.

She was appointed because she was the only person in the room who could produce a list of every holder of federal paper in the eastern half of the country. That list is still the foundation of the Corridor, and it is the reason the section has more money than anyone and no friends at all.

She has no farmland. This is the Corridor in one sentence. It has eight times the population of the Lakes Union and roughly a tenth of the food, and every day the arithmetic gets worse, and she has solved it the only way it can be solved, which is by importing everything and being the most indispensable creditor in America.

What people get wrong: they assume she is a monster because of the loans. She has, in fact, never raised a rate, never called in an early default, and written off a hundred million dollars of Corridor paper owed by families that could not pay it. She did all of this on purpose and it cost her leverage she still misses.

What she does not know how to do: be liked. She has tried to learn twice and both times it read as a tactic, and she has concluded the problem is her, and she has stopped trying.

### First meeting

*The meeting is in a borrowed room above a shipping office in Philadelphia, booked under a freight company that does not exist. Yvonne is already seated when you arrive, and she is not reading anything. There is a carafe and one paper cup, which becomes two when she pours for you without being asked.*

**Yvonne Castille:** Thank you for coming. I know the drive. I am sorry about the drive, and I would like to say something about that before we start, because I think the thing people find hardest about me is that I would like to say things about the drive.

**Player:** My lord.

**Yvonne Castille:** Ms. Castille, or Madame, or nothing at all. There is a woman in this building right now who can buy your village and she calls me Your Excellency, and I would prefer not to start from a place where you think I have power. I would like to start from a place where I am asking.

**Player:** Then ask.

**Yvonne Castille:** I am going to describe a situation to you. Please interrupt me, because I have been told that I do this for eleven minutes without breathing and I believe the criticism.

**Player:** Go on.

**Yvonne Castille:** The Corridor has the largest population of any section and the least food. Not slightly less, not a shortage, structurally none. We grow what New Jersey grows. We have eight and a half million people inside a hundred miles of water and the water does not contain food. Everything on my tables arrives from somewhere else, and if the somewhere else stops, the tables get shorter.

**Player:** That is a war you cannot win with soldiers.

**Yvonne Castille:** No. It is not. Thank you, that is exactly the right first thing to say, and most people start with a speech about how we are the greatest civilization in the history of the world, which is a thing people say when they are losing an argument.

*She refills her own cup. It is a small, deliberate, unhurried movement.*

**Yvonne Castille:** Here is what I do about it. I lend. Not out of generosity, out of position. If a state cannot grow what it eats, it borrows. If it cannot repay in money, it repays in access, ports, rail, standing capacity. Over six years I have put myself between the Corridor and every other section's ability to feed itself, and every one of those sections knows it, and not one of them is stupid enough to say so out loud.

**Player:** So they hate you.

**Yvonne Castille:** They do not hate me. Hatred would be a relief. They are courteous, which is worse, and they wait, and they are waiting right now for a bad harvest I have already priced in.

**Player:** What do you want from me?

**Yvonne Castille:** Something I cannot ask anyone else for, which is why I have spent a year thinking about it and about six minutes actually saying it.

*She sets the cup down.*

**Yvonne Castille:** The Corridor is not poor, it is not weak, and it is not disorganized. It has one problem and it is not a food problem. It is that I am the only person in the eastern half of this country who can write a check, and the day I am not, this section loses every negotiation it is in within a month. I have been the credit. I have been the office. I have been the woman who answers.

**Player:** And now?

**Yvonne Castille:** Now I am fifty-seven and I have noticed that a system where one person is load bearing is not a system, it is a delay. There is a plan to distribute this and it is a serious plan with serious people attached to it, and none of them are you yet, and I would rather you had two years to become boring and dependable than have any of them find you in six months.

**Player:** So this is a test.

**Yvonne Castille:** It is a job with a test built into it, which is the only kind of job there is. You will do unglamorous work for a while. You will build a relationship with a governor in Buffalo or Providence who currently despises me and would never admit it to my face. You will carry a message that is not important and make it feel important. And in two years, if you are still here and I am gone, the Corridor will still eat.

**Player:** And if you are not gone?

**Yvonne Castille:** Then you will have spent two years learning the single most valuable thing anyone can learn in this country, which is how the money actually moves, and I will have bought the best insurance available, which is a man who knows where the switch is.

*She almost smiles. It is a small thing and it does not reach her eyes, and it is the most human moment she has.*

**Yvonne Castille:** I want to be fair with you about the compensation, since the Compensation is my whole personality and you should know what you are getting into. The Corridor pays the best rates in North America and always will. You will be able to buy a house this year. If you come to me with a specific number, I can usually meet it.

**Player:** That is very generous.

**Yvonne Castille:** It is a wage, and I will also make you a loan, and the loan is the part you should think about. The wage is the wage. The loan is a relationship, and relationships have terms, and the terms are not hidden from you because a hidden term is a trap I would have to be present for later and I will not be, because I will be dead or retired.

**Player:** That is either very honest or very clever.

**Yvonne Castille:** It is honest. I have been clever for twenty-two years and clever is what got me here, and it has not gotten me a single person in my own building who is not on my payroll. I have decided to try being honest instead and see how it goes, and I do not know yet whether it works.

*She stands, which means the meeting is ending, and does not offer a hand.*

**Yvonne Castille:** One question, and then I will let you go, and I would like an honest answer rather than a good one.

**Player:** Ask.

**Yvonne Castille:** If the Corridor ran out of money tomorrow, and I am gone, and the people who depend on imports are hungry, what would you do? Would you hold the city, would you open the warehouses, or would you leave?

*She waits, and she is not impatient, and she does not help you.*

**Player:** ...

**Yvonne Castille:** Take your time. That answer is going to be true in about two years and I would rather we both knew it now.

### Notes for implementation

- The least likable leader and the most necessary one. Her quest line is administrative rather than martial, and the player should be able to complete it while fighting almost nothing.
- Calculation 5, Generosity 1, Valor 1. She is the natural counter to a player who wants to solve problems with cavalry.
- Her loans are the game's best economic content and its worst trap. Both of those are true at once, which is her whole design.

## 7. WHAT ALL SIX HAVE IN COMMON

The player will meet all six, in person, and every one of these conversations needs to feel different in the mouth. The structural rules:

1. **Every leader offers the player their faction's actual problem, not a quest.** Vega needs grain on roads, Grant needs a fighter in the east, Oyelaran needs to be believed in a cornfield, Jessup needs a chaperone, Adair needs distance handled, Castille needs a successor. None of them is asking for anything a player would call exciting.
2. **Every leader's first offer can be refused.** Refusing must not lock content, only shape it. A player who says no to all six is not softlocked, they are a Wanderer with six grudges and better information than anyone.
3. **Every leader is wrong about one thing.** Vega is wrong that courage is unnecessary. Grant is wrong that he can keep the Alliance loose. Oyelaran is wrong that fairness survives a famine. Jessup is wrong that everyone can be managed. Adair is wrong that money is enough. Castille is wrong that honesty is a substitute for being liked. These are the flaws the player gets to exploit or protect, and they are where the late-game content lives.
4. **They should be able to be replaced.** Each of them has at least one viable path where the player takes a section by force, guile, or election, and each replaced leader should have a death or an exile that means something to the section that loses them.
5. **They should be able to be defeated morally rather than militarily.** The Corridor's food problem is solved by its own leadership failing to solve it. That is a valid win condition.
