# LORE.md

The world bible: America, divided. This is the canon everything else builds on —
quests, rumors, endings, ruler ambitions, and dialogue all read from here.

**Status:** canon. Changing anything in sections 1–5 means updating quests,
endings, and faction dialogue that quote it. Sections 6–9 describe systems, not
story, and stay in sync with `RULERS.md`, `ERA.md`, and `FACTIONS.md`.

**Canon rules (from `CONSTITUTION.md`):** real geography and real city names are
used as-is. Every event, faction, leader, and history below is fictional — the
game never replays real wars, dates, or crises. No real person appears as a
character. Weapons, vehicles, and gear use generic or fictional names.

---

## 1. THE FRACTURE

No single day broke America. That is the first thing the old ones get wrong,
and the last thing they stop arguing about.

What happened was slower and meaner than a war. The federal government did not
fall; it thinned. Money dried up first — then mandates went unfunded, then
agencies stopped answering their phones, then the highway money stopped coming
and nobody in Washington could explain why. States started making their own
deals because waiting had become a kind of dying. A water compact here. A power
grid agreement there. A port authority that began collecting its own tariffs
"temporarily" and never stopped.

Trade corridors needed guards. Guards needed pay. Pay needed taxes. Taxes
needed authority. Authority, it turned out, was something you could build
yourself out of a radio tower, a fuel depot, and enough armed men to hold both.

The six sections were never declared. They accumulated — deal by deal, depot by
depot — until one morning the maps in the truck stops showed borders that no
legislature had voted for and every convoy driver already obeyed. The old flag
still flies over the Atlantic Corridor's government buildings. Nobody can quite
say what it governs anymore.

This is why the game can start in any decade from 1950 to 2009 (`ERA.md`): the
Fracture is not an event with a date. It is a condition that deepened. In 1950
the sections are young compacts, still telling themselves this is temporary. By
2009 they are old countries that have forgotten they were ever otherwise.

## 2. THE SIX SECTIONS

### The Pacific Compact

*California, Oregon, Washington, Hawaii, Alaska.*

The Compact was born in the ports. When the federal customs service went hollow,
the harbor masters of Los Angeles, San Francisco, Seattle, and Portland did the
arithmetic themselves: the ships were still coming, the tariffs were still
collectable, and whoever controlled the docks controlled the money. They formed
a compact to split the revenue and keep the cranes running. The tech firms came
later, drawn by the ports and the power, and the Compact learned to sell
intelligence the way it sold berth space — quietly, and at a premium.

It is the richest section per mile of coastline and the hungriest per acre of
farmland. The Compact's cities eat food they do not grow, shipped in over
mountain passes and desert highways that belong, technically, to other people.
Every Compact ruler knows the arithmetic by heart: cut the inland supply and
the great coastal cities go hungry in weeks. So the Compact pays — for grain,
for water rights, for the loyalty of the inland counties that resent the coast
and feed it anyway. Coastal cities and inland counties disagree about
everything except the price of bread.

**The way of war:** marksmen, engineers, medics. Fewer, better-equipped
soldiers — the Compact cannot afford to waste people it cannot replace, so it
spends money instead of lives. Its signature is the *Port Trade*: ports add
income, but a blockaded or raided port wounds the whole section at once.

**The fault line:** water. Rulers quarrel over rivers the way old nations
quarreled over oil. The Compact is rich, well-fed on paper, and one bad harvest
inland away from finding out what its money cannot buy.

### The Mountain Alliance

*Montana, Idaho, Wyoming, Utah, Colorado, Nevada, Arizona, New Mexico.*

The Alliance is eight states that agreed on exactly one thing: nobody from
outside tells them what to do. Mine owners, ranchers, and the governors of
empty counties formed a mutual-defense pact against what they called "lowland
appetites" — first for water, then for minerals, then for everything. The
Alliance never built a capital worth the name. Its rulers meet where the roads
allow and go home heavily armed.

It holds the continent's richest gold and metal ground, and the emptiest
country. The math of the Alliance is brutal: you can hold ground nobody can
take, and starve holding it. There are not enough people here to replace what
you lose, so Alliance wars are fought the way mountain people have always
fought them — from above, from cover, and only when the ground itself is on
your side. Attackers lose speed and take attrition in the high country. The
Alliance's rulers are famously independent, which means the section resists
being conquered piece by piece and resists being *led* just as hard.

**The way of war:** scouts, mountain infantry, snipers. The signature is *High
Ground*: enormous defensive advantage and attacker attrition in rough terrain,
bought with almost no ability to project force outward.

**The fault line:** unity. The leader of the Alliance has less authority over
its member rulers than any other section head on the map. Food and water are
scarce, settlements are far apart, and every ruler with a mine thinks they are
the Alliance.

### The Great Lakes Union

*North Dakota, South Dakota, Nebraska, Kansas, Iowa, Minnesota, Wisconsin,
Michigan, Illinois, Indiana, Ohio, Missouri.*

The Union feeds the continent and knows it. When the Fracture came, the farm
states looked at their grain elevators, their factories, and their numbers —
the largest pool of recruits in America — and made the coldest calculation on
the map: everyone needs to eat, so everyone will deal. The Union sells food the
way other sections sell loyalty, and it has never once been shy about the
leverage.

Its cities are Chicago, Detroit, Minneapolis, Cleveland, Columbus, Kansas City,
St. Louis — industrial muscle wrapped around the breadbasket. The Union's
weakness is the twin of its strength: everyone comes for the grain. Sell it all
for money and money cannot be eaten; keep it all and every neighbor declares
war over it. Member rulers argue endlessly over selling versus stockpiling, and
a bad harvest can split the section faster than any invasion. The land is flat
and open — hard to defend, easy to march across, which everyone has noticed.

**The way of war:** heavy infantry, large militia levies, mechanics. Mass and
industry: the Union fields more bodies and more machines than anyone and dares
you to do the same. Its signature is *Bread and Iron*: food exports buy
alliances and gold, but selling too much leaves nothing in reserve when the
harvest fails.

**The fault line:** appetite. The Union is constantly targeted and constantly
courted, and it is not always careful about which is which.

### The Southern Compact

*Kentucky, Tennessee, Arkansas, Louisiana, Mississippi, Alabama, Georgia,
Florida, South Carolina, North Carolina.*

The Southern Compact mobilized first and asked questions never. When order
thinned, the South's governors, guard commanders, and county strongmen already
had the men, the rifles, and the habit of mustering. The Compact formalized
what existed: large, ready militaries, cheap recruits, and the fastest
mobilization on the continent. Atlanta, Miami, Charlotte, Nashville, New
Orleans, Jacksonville — big cities with long growing seasons and full armories.

Southern armies gather quicker than anyone else's. They also go home quicker
when the money runs out, because the Compact's tax base is the thinnest of the
six and long wars are a luxury it cannot afford. Its rulers quarrel and hold
grudges as a matter of style; vassal loyalty is harder to keep here than
anywhere, and lords have been known to refuse orders mid-campaign over a slight
from three years back. Storms and heat hammer the section regularly, stressing
food and disease systems that are always one bad season from crisis.

**The way of war:** light infantry, riflemen, mounted scouts. Speed and mass:
raise an army faster than anyone, hit hard, and finish before the treasury
notices. Its signature is the *Call to Arms*: quick, cheap mobilization — but
lords may refuse orders if loyalty or old grievances say so.

**The fault line:** money and memory. You can raise an army faster than anyone
and go bankrupt doing it, and the men you raised will remember who failed to
pay them longer than they remember who they fought.

### The Lone Star Frontier

*Texas, Oklahoma.*

The Frontier did not join anything. It looked at its energy fields, its
industry, its farmland, and its border with everybody, and decided it was
already a country. Texas and Oklahoma formalized the obvious: a compact of two
that answers to no one, trades with everyone, and keeps the fastest horses and
the longest rifles on the map. Houston, Dallas, San Antonio, Austin, Oklahoma
City — sprawling, rich, and very far apart.

Frontier riders are legend: mounted riflemen and rangers who can cross
enormous distances and hit supply lines like weather. But distance is the bill.
Every march from home costs more food and more money than the same march
anywhere else, the borders touch many rivals with few natural allies, and the
sprawling cities are hard to garrison evenly. The Frontier is independent by
nature, with fewer internal factions than other sides — which means when it
does split, it splits clean in half.

**The way of war:** mounted riflemen, rangers, ranch militia. The signature is
*Long Reach*: the fastest cavalry and the best raiders on the continent, paid
for in food and money every mile from home.

**The fault line:** distance. The Frontier can strike anyone and hold nothing
cheaply.

### The Atlantic Corridor

*Maine, New Hampshire, Vermont, Massachusetts, Rhode Island, Connecticut,
New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Delaware, Maryland, D.C., Virginia,
West Virginia.*

The Corridor is what remains of the old order, and it knows exactly what that
is worth. When Washington thinned, the money did not leave — the banks, the
ports, the gold reserves, and the habits of power stayed exactly where they
were. The Corridor hosts what remains of the federal institutions, flies the
old flag, and runs the continent's deepest ledgers. New York, Philadelphia,
Boston, Washington, Baltimore, Pittsburgh: the richest banks and the biggest
population in America.

It can pay for anything and it cannot feed itself. Almost no farmland, dense
cities where outbreaks spread fastest, and a whole continent of debtors and
rivals who either owe it money or resent it — often both. The Corridor's genius
is that it turned this into a weapon: loans, debts, and bribes on a scale no
other section can match. Its diplomats and intelligence officers are the best
in the game, because they have to be. A road cut out there is a famine, and
everyone the Corridor owns is waiting for it.

**The way of war:** professional soldiers, medics, intelligence officers.
Discipline and information: the Corridor wins by knowing more and breaking
later. Its signature is the *Ledger*: debts and deals other sides cannot match
— but a debt-heavy position can be turned against its holder.

**The fault line:** hunger. All the money in America cannot be eaten, and the
Corridor's enemies do the arithmetic every single day.

## 3. THE SPACES BETWEEN

The map is not six colors edge to edge. Between the sections run the gray
places: dead interstates, dry counties, towns the compacts tax but do not
garrison, and stretches where the only law is whoever is armed today.

**Bandits and raider leaders** (`RULERS.md`) operate where law is weak. They
are not an faction with a flag; they are a condition of the roads. A medicine
caravan robbed on a gray highway means a clinic runs dry, an outbreak spreads,
workers die, farms go unharvested, food runs short, and unrest rises until a
town turns on its ruler. That chain is not scripted — it is what happens when
independent systems collide (`CAUSE_EFFECT.md`, `README.md`).

**Mercenary captains** sell their parties to the highest bidder and their
loyalty to no one. Every section hires them; every section has been burned by
them. A mercenary company that changes sides mid-campaign is not a betrayal
mechanic. It is Tuesday.

**Unclaimed towns** sit in the gray places with their own mayors, militias, and
prices. They trade with whoever shows up armed and polite, and they remember
who showed up armed and otherwise.

## 4. THE CURRENCY OF POWER

Four resources run the world (`ECONOMY.md`). The lore reason is simple: in a
fractured America, these are the four things you cannot fake.

- **Food** is life on a timer. Cities eat every day. A side that cannot feed
  its people does not get a rebellion — it gets an empty city, because the
  people walk away or die where they stand.
- **Metal** is war made physical. Rifles, trucks, rails, wire, spare parts.
  The Mountain Alliance sits on it; everyone else buys, steals, or captures it.
- **Money** is trust with a printing press. It pays wages, buys grain, hires
  mercenaries, and bribes lords. It is also the first thing to die in a siege —
  nobody takes paper from a ruler who might not exist next month.
- **Gold** is trust without the press. When money dies, gold talks. The
  Corridor hoards it, the Alliance mines it, and every desperate ruler in
  between has a chest of it buried somewhere.

Take a city and fail to feed it, and the council votes you out. That is not a
rule in a design doc. That is how power works here.

## 5. FIFTY YEARS OF WAR

The campaign can start anywhere from 1950 to 2009, and war looks nothing alike
at the ends of that span (`ERA.md`). This is the lived history:

- **1950s:** Bolt-actions, early semi-autos, trucks and jeeps. Field phones
  and newspapers. Wars are fought by men who can see each other, supplied by
  roads that flood every spring. Medicine is basic; a gut wound is a death
  sentence and everyone knows it.
- **1960s–1970s:** Assault rifles, machine guns, rocket launchers. Armored
  carriers, better tanks. Radio and television: now the home front watches, and
  rulers learn that losing looks different on a screen. Wiretaps teach the
  Corridor's intelligence officers their trade.
- **1980s:** Improved rifles, night vision, anti-tank missiles. Mechanized
  infantry. Field radios in every squad; early computers in every headquarters.
  Wars get faster, louder, and harder to hide.
- **1990s–2000s:** Optics, precision weapons, fast mechanized forces. GPS,
  mobile phones, the internet. A ruler in 2005 can watch a convoy move in near
  real time — and so can their enemy. Information becomes a weapon the way
  grain was, and the Pacific Compact and Atlantic Corridor, the two sections
  built on knowing things, become the most dangerous players on the map.

Technology is a table, not a script: a rich side adopts early, a poor side
adopts late, and a clever side captures what it cannot buy. A 1950 Great Lakes
levy with bolt-actions and a 2005 one with optics are the same section, the
same pride, and a completely different army.

## 6. HOW POWER WORKS

There are roughly three hundred to eight hundred named rulers in America at any
time (`RULERS.md`), generated — not hand-written — from the real shape of the
country: a side leader per section, governors per state, city rulers and
mayors, lords and clan heads holding towns and villages, militia captains,
mercenary captains, bandit leaders. Every one of them has a name (fictional,
culturally plausible, never a real living person), a family, traits, skills,
holdings, wealth, relations, ambitions, and a loyalty that can be bought,
earned, or broken.

Power flows the way it always has: the side leader declares the wars, the
governors hold the states, the lords hold the towns, and everyone in between
negotiates upward and steals downward. The player can rise through every tier —
or skip them entirely, the way conquerors do.

Succession is where sections bleed. A dead side leader with no clear heir does
not cause a cutscene. It causes three governors to stop answering orders, two
lords to declare for different nephews, and a mercenary company to triple its
rates. The game does not script this. It just stops enforcing the fiction that
anyone was ever fully in charge.

## 7. THE WANDERER

Every section has a story about the kind of person who starts with nothing.
The Compact calls them salvage. The Alliance calls them drift. The Union calls
them harvest hands. The South calls them trouble. The Frontier calls them
prospects. The Corridor has a form for them, seventeen pages, most of it about
debt.

The Wanderer starts as a nobody, anywhere on the map. No side, no bonuses, no
penalties, no one coming. It is the closest thing to the old world's freedom:
join any side later, build your own, or never belong to anything at all. Most
wanderers die unknown on gray highways. The ones who don't become the stories
the sections tell about themselves.

## 8. ENDINGS

There is no single victory screen, because there was never a single war. What
"winning" means depends on what was built (`content/lore/endings.md` expands
each of these into full quest chains):

- **The Unifier:** hold a majority of America under one banner long enough
  that the maps stop showing the old borders. Nobody has done it. Everybody
  has tried.
- **The Kingmaker:** never take the top seat — put rulers on every throne and
  own their debts. The Corridor's favorite game, playable by anyone with
  enough gold and patience.
- **The Breadlord:** control the food. In a hungry country, the hand that
  feeds is the hand that rules, and it never has to draw a weapon.
- **The Ghost:** win without a country. A mercenary company so feared and so
  rich that sections pay it not to march. Power without territory, and all the
  enemies that come with it.
- **The Survivor:** keep one town alive through everything — famine, plague,
  siege, betrayal — for a full generation. The smallest ending. The hardest
  one.

## 9. CANON RULES

What is fixed and what is generated, so future writers do not break the world:

1. **Fixed:** the six sections, their states, their cultures, their fault
   lines, the Fracture as a slow unraveling (never a dated event), the
   1950–2009 span, the four currencies of power.
2. **Generated:** all named rulers, families, ambitions, and relations
   (`RULERS.md` pipeline). Never hand-write a ruler into canon.
3. **Fictional, always:** every event, leader, and history is invented. Real
   geography and city names are used as-is. No real person appears. Item names
   stay generic or fictional.
4. **Grit, not cute:** the tone of this world is weathered cloth and iron, not
   candy (`clients/campaign/src/design/factions.ts`). Nothing in the lore
   undercuts that.
5. **Cause, not script:** lore explains *why* systems behave the way they do.
   It never invents outcomes the systems cannot produce. If the lore says the
   Compact starves when its supply is cut, that is because the food system
   actually starves it.

## 10. CITIES OF THE DIVIDED STATES

Every city below is real geography with fictional history. The game uses real
names and real positions; everything that happened there is invented.

### Chicago — The Breadbasket Throne

The Great Lakes Union's beating heart and the largest grain exchange on the
continent. The old Board of Trade building still stands, but the trading floor
is now a war room where Union quartermasters decide who eats. Chicago's rulers
have ended three wars without firing a shot — by closing the elevators.

The city runs on a simple threat: the Union feeds everyone, and everyone
knows it. Chicago's docks load grain onto barges that go everywhere, and the
harbor master's signature is worth more than most governors' oaths. The
stockyards are gone, replaced by motor pools and militia barracks. The El
still runs, carrying workers to the factories that build the Union's trucks
and rifles.

**What the streets say:** "Chicago doesn't need walls. Hunger does the work."

**Quest hook:** A Union quartermaster offers you a contract — escort a grain
convoy through gray territory to a starving Corridor city. The pay is enormous.
The catch: two other sections have offered more to make sure it never arrives.

### New York — The Ledger

The Atlantic Corridor's crown and the continent's bank. Wall Street never
stopped trading; it just stopped pretending the money was American. Now it's
Corridor scrip, Union grain notes, Compact tech bonds, and gold — always gold.

Manhattan is a fortress of glass and old stone, guarded by the Corridor's best
professionals. The outer boroughs are a patchwork of neighborhoods that pay
protection to whoever holds the nearest bridge. The port is the busiest on the
eastern seaboard, and every ship is inspected three times — once by the harbor
authority, once by Corridor intelligence, and once by whoever bribed both.

**What the streets say:** "In New York, everything is for sale. Even the
things that aren't."

**Quest hook:** A Corridor banker wants you to recover a ledger from a
defaulted debtor lord in the gray places. The ledger contains debts owed by
half the rulers in America. Everyone wants it. No one wants you to have it.

### Los Angeles — The Port of Lies

The Pacific Compact's southern anchor and the most beautiful trap on the map.
LA's ports handle more cargo than any three other Compact harbors combined,
and the city's rulers have turned that into an art form: tariffs, fees,
inspections, delays — all negotiable, all expensive, all deniable.

The city sprawls across basins and hills, a patchwork of walled studio
compounds (now mercenary headquarters), port authority fortresses, and
neighborhoods that change hands with the tides. The old film industry is gone,
but the talent for fiction remains — LA's diplomats are the best liars in the
Compact, and that's saying something.

**What the streets say:** "LA will sell you the harbor, the ships, and the
water they're floating on. Read the contract."

**Quest hook:** A Compact harbor master needs a "deniable" solution to a
rival port's blockade. The job pays in berth rights — worth a fortune, if you
live to collect.

### Houston — The Energy Throne

The Lone Star Frontier's beating heart and the continent's fuel pump. Houston's
refineries never stopped, and the Frontier's rulers understood before anyone
else that in a fractured America, energy is sovereignty.

The city is a sprawl of refineries, port facilities, and walled company towns.
The old energy majors are gone, replaced by Frontier-chartered combines that
answer to Austin and pay in fuel. Houston's mechanics can fix anything with an
engine, and its rangers can ride from the Gulf to the Panhandle without
stopping.

**What the streets say:** "Houston doesn't negotiate. It sets the price."

**Quest hook:** A refinery combine needs escort for a fuel convoy to a
besieged Frontier outpost. The route crosses three sections' territory. The
pay is in fuel — which is to say, in power.

### Atlanta — The Muster Ground

The Southern Compact's military heart and the fastest mobilization point on
the continent. Atlanta's armories can put ten thousand rifles in hands in a
week, and the city's rulers have done it more than once.

The city is a fortress of barracks, training grounds, and supply depots wrapped
around a civilian core that exists to feed the machine. Every Southern lord
sends their sons here to learn war, and every Southern war starts here.

**What the streets say:** "Atlanta doesn't start wars. It just makes them
possible."

**Quest hook:** A Southern lord needs you to muster a company in 72 hours for
a border war. The recruits are willing. The rifles are... mostly there. The
paymaster is definitely lying about the bonus.

### St. Louis — The Crossroads

Where the Great Lakes Union meets the Southern Compact meets the gray places.
St. Louis is a trading city that has been conquered, liberated, and
"protected" by everyone, and it has learned to survive by being useful to
whoever holds the bridges.

The Arch still stands, a monument to a country that no longer exists. Below
it, the riverfront is a chaos of barges, mercenary recruiters, and spies. Every
section has agents here. None of them trust each other. All of them do business.

**What the streets say:** "St. Louis belongs to whoever paid last. Check the
receipts."

**Quest hook:** Three different employers want you to secure the same river
crossing. They're all paying. They're all lying about why.

### Denver — The High Vault

The Mountain Alliance's de facto capital (though no Alliance ruler would admit
to having a capital). Denver sits at the edge of the high country, guarding
the passes and the mines beyond.

The city is a fortress by geography — high, dry, and hard to approach without
being seen. The old mint still holds gold, and the Alliance's rulers meet in
the brownstones downtown to argue about water rights and mine shares. Everyone
is armed. No one is polite.

**What the streets say:** "In Denver, the altitude isn't the only thing
that'll kill you."

**Quest hook:** An Alliance mine owner needs you to escort a gold shipment
through the high passes. The route is safe from bandits — it's the other
Alliance rulers you need to worry about.

### Seattle — The Northern Port

The Pacific Compact's northern anchor, where the timber meets the sea. Seattle's
harbor masters control the lumber trade, the fishing fleets, and the northern
shipping lanes.

The city is damp, gray, and rich. The old tech money is gone, replaced by port
revenue and intelligence contracts. Seattle's spies are the best in the Compact
— patient, quiet, and absolutely ruthless.

**What the streets say:** "Seattle doesn't sleep. It watches."

**Quest hook:** A Compact spymaster needs you to extract an asset from a
Corridor safehouse. The asset knows where the bodies are buried. Literally.

## 11. LEGENDS AND RUMORS

Stories the wasteland tells itself. Some are true. All are useful.

### The Ghost Convoy

Every section has a version. A supply convoy that vanished in the gray places
— no wreckage, no bodies, no witnesses. Just empty road and a radio that
broadcasts static on the old emergency frequency. Drivers who hear it turn
around. The ones who don't... don't come back.

**The truth:** No one knows. That's the point.

### The Bread King of Chicago

They say there's a quartermaster in Chicago who ended the Third Grain War by
himself. No battles, no treaties — just a signature on a manifest that rerouted
twelve grain barges. Three armies starved. One city ate. He was never seen
again, but every Union ruler keeps a chair empty at the table. Just in case.

### The Denver Vault

The old mint in Denver supposedly holds gold reserves from before the Fracture
— billions in bullion, guarded by the Alliance's best. Every few years someone
tries to rob it. No one has succeeded. The bodies are displayed as a warning.

**The truth:** The vault is real. The gold is real. The guards are better than
you think.

### The Atlanta Muster

They say a Southern lord once mustered an army of fifty thousand in three days.
No one believes it. Everyone prepares for it.

### The New York Ledger

There's a book in Manhattan that contains every debt owed by every ruler in
America. Whoever holds it could bankrupt three sections or buy a fourth. It's
been stolen seventeen times. It's never left the island.

### The Wanderer's Road

A gray highway in the Midwest where wanderers — the nobodies, the drifters,
the ones who start with nothing — supposedly find their destiny. Travelers
leave offerings at a truck stop: a bullet, a coin, a photograph. No one knows
who started it. Everyone does it.

## 12. THE MERCENARY COMPANIES

Soldiers for hire. Loyalty for sale. Every section uses them; every section
has been burned by them.

### The Gray Jackals

The largest and most feared company on the continent. Five thousand rifles,
veterans of every section's wars. They'll fight for anyone who pays — and
they've changed sides mid-battle three times that anyone can prove.

**Reputation:** Expensive. Effective. Untrustworthy.
**Price:** More than you can afford. Less than losing.
**Warning:** Never hire them to guard something you can't afford to lose.

### The Harvest Hands

Union-born, farm-raised, and utterly reliable — as long as the pay is in grain
or gold. The Hands specialize in defensive contracts: guard this town, hold
this bridge, protect this convoy. They've never broken a defensive contract.
Offensive work... costs extra.

**Reputation:** Solid. Honest. Boring (which is why they're alive).
**Price:** Fair. In grain, preferably.
**Warning:** Don't ask them to attack. They'll do it, but they'll hate you.

### The Port Rats

Compact marines gone freelance. Experts in amphibious assaults, port seizures,
and "deniable" harbor operations. If a port changes hands overnight and no one
claims responsibility, it was the Rats.

**Reputation:** Sneaky. Effective. Gone by morning.
**Price:** High. In port rights or tech.
**Warning:** Count your cargo after they leave.

### The High Country

Alliance mountain infantry who hired out when the mines went dry. The best
scouts and snipers for hire, utterly unbeatable in rough terrain. Useless in
cities — they get lost and cranky.

**Reputation:** The best at what they do. Only do one thing.
**Price:** Moderate. In food and ammunition.
**Warning:** Don't deploy them in flat country. They'll sulk.

### The Ledger Men

Corridor professionals — ex-intelligence, ex-military, all business. They don't
fight wars; they end them. Sabotage, assassination, "negotiation." If a ruler
dies of "natural causes" at an inconvenient time, check who hired the Ledger Men.

**Reputation:** Terrifying. Discreet. Final.
**Price:** Astronomical. In gold, up front.
**Warning:** Never meet them twice.

## 13. DAILY LIFE IN THE DIVIDED STATES

What it's actually like to live here, for the people who aren't rulers.

### In the Cities

City dwellers live behind walls — sometimes stone, sometimes wire, sometimes
just the reputation of the garrison. They work in factories, docks, markets,
or the endless bureaucracy of section government. Food comes from the Union
or the surrounding farmland, and everyone knows the price of bread to the
penny — it's the difference between calm and riot.

Children go to school if the city can afford it. Most can. The Corridor's
schools are the best, the Compact's are the most practical, the Union's are
the most numerous. The Frontier's schools teach riding alongside reading.
The South's schools teach history as a warning. The Alliance's schools...
are far apart.

### In the Towns

Town life revolves around the market, the militia muster, and the mayor's
whims. Most towns have a wall or a palisade, a well or a cistern, and a
militia that drills on Sundays. The tavern is the news service, the hiring
hall, and the court of public opinion.

Farmers farm. Smiths smith. Traders trade. Everyone watches the road, because
the road brings news, money, and trouble — often in that order.

### On the Roads

The gray highways are the arteries of the divided states, and they're dangerous.
Convoys travel armed. Lone travelers travel fast or not at all. Truck stops
are neutral ground by ancient custom — even bandits respect the truce, because
everyone needs fuel and no one wants to be the one who broke it.

Bandits are a fact of life, like weather. You prepare, you pay protection, or
you risk it. Mercenaries guard the rich convoys. The poor convoys pray.

### The Seasons

**Spring:** Planting. Every section holds its breath — a good planting means
a good harvest means a quiet summer. A bad planting means... calculations.

**Summer:** Campaign season. Armies march when the roads are dry and the food
is growing. Wars start in summer.

**Autumn:** Harvest. The most dangerous season — everyone's watching the
yields, everyone's doing arithmetic, and a bad harvest can start a war faster
than any insult.

**Winter:** The quiet season. Armies go home (or starve). Rulers scheme.
Everyone waits for spring.

## 14. FAMOUS BATTLES

Wars that shaped the map. Every ruler studies them. Every soldier fears
repeating them.

### The Third Grain War (Union vs. Corridor, 1978)

The Corridor tried to break the Union's food monopoly by seizing the Mississippi
grain barges. The Union responded by closing every elevator for a month. Three
Corridor cities starved. The Corridor paid triple for peace and never tried it
again.

**Lesson:** Don't fight the people who feed you.

### The Denver Standoff (Alliance vs. Everyone, 1985)

Four sections sent armies to take the Denver gold vault. The Alliance didn't
fight them — it just closed the passes and waited. Winter did the rest. The
armies went home. The gold stayed.

**Lesson:** The mountains are the Alliance's army.

### The Atlanta Muster (South vs. Frontier, 1992)

The South mustered 40,000 men in five days and marched on Frontier oil fields.
The Frontier didn't meet them — it burned the fields and retreated, then
harassed the Southern supply lines until the army starved. The South went home.
The fields were rebuilt in a year.

**Lesson:** Speed is not supply.

### The Port War (Compact vs. Corridor, 2001)

The Corridor tried to blockade Compact ports. The Compact's Port Rats seized
Corridor harbors in retaliation. Both sides lost ships. Neither side won.
They signed a treaty that both sides still cheat on.

**Lesson:** Ports are hard to hold and easy to lose.

## 15. TRADE GOODS OF THE DIVIDED STATES

What moves on the roads, and why it matters.

### Grain

The Union's weapon and everyone's necessity. Wheat, corn, soy — the stuff of
life. Price spikes cause wars. Price drops cause different wars.

### Fuel

The Frontier's power. Refined petroleum for trucks, generators, and war
machines. Without it, armies walk.

### Metal

The Alliance's wealth. Iron, steel, copper — the bones of civilization. Every
rifle, every truck, every rail needs it.

### Medicine

The Compact's edge. Antibiotics, surgical supplies, vaccines. In a world of
war and disease, medicine is worth more than gold.

### Gold

The Corridor's hoard. Universal currency when trust fails. Every ruler has
some buried. No one admits how much.

### Tech

The Compact's trade. Radios, computers, optics, intelligence. Information as
a commodity.

### Weapons

Everyone's business. Rifles, ammunition, parts. The gray market never closes.

### Horses

The Frontier's pride. Fast, tough, and essential where fuel is scarce. A good
horse is worth ten rifles.

## 16. BELIEFS AND SUPERSTITIONS

What people believe when the old certainties are gone.

### The Old Flag

The Atlantic Corridor still flies the Stars and Stripes. Most people under 40
have never lived in the America it represented, but the flag means something:
order, memory, the idea that things were once whole. Corridor citizens salute
it. Others spit when they see it. Everyone knows what it means.

### The Road Saints

Truckers and travelers pray to saints of the highway — St. Christopher (still
popular), the Ghost Driver (who guides lost convoys), the Mechanic (who fixes
what can't be fixed). Offerings at truck stops: a bolt, a coin, a cigarette.

### The Harvest Rite

Union farming towns hold a harvest festival every autumn — part thanksgiving,
part warning. If the harvest is good, they celebrate. If it's bad, they pray.
Everyone understands the subtext: we eat because the land allows it.

### The Muster Oath

Southern soldiers swear on mustering: "I go where I'm sent, I fight where I'm
put, I come home when I'm told." Breaking the oath means exile. No one breaks
it twice.

### The High Silence

Alliance mountain folk don't talk about the dead. They build cairns on the
passes and walk on. Speech is for the living.

## 17. QUEST SEEDS

Story hooks for wanderers, rulers, and everyone in between. Each is a starting
point, not a script.

### The Missing Quartermaster

A Union grain convoy never arrived. The quartermaster is missing. The grain is
missing. Three sections blame each other. Find the truth — if you can survive
it.

### The Denver Job

Someone wants the Denver gold vault robbed. They've assembled a crew. They need
a leader. The pay is... unimaginable. The odds are worse.

### The Plague Ship

A Compact freighter arrived in port with fever aboard. The harbor master
quarantined it. Now the crew is dying, the city is panicking, and someone needs
to go aboard and find out what happened.

### The Deserter

A Southern lord's best captain deserted with a company of rifles. The lord wants
him back — alive, if possible. The captain has a reason. It's a good one.

### The Election

A gray town is holding an election for mayor. Two candidates. One is backed by
the Union, one by the Corridor. Both are lying. The town just wants clean water.

### The Caravan

A merchant needs escort through bandit country. Simple job. Good pay. Except
the cargo isn't what the manifest says, and the bandits aren't the only ones
hunting it.

### The Heir

A dying ruler has no clear successor. Three claimants. One is competent, one is
ruthless, one is... you, if you play it right.

### The Ghost

A mercenary company was wiped out to the last man. Except one survived. He's
been hunting the ones who betrayed them, one by one. He needs help with the
last one.

## 18. THE FIVE ENDINGS — FULL QUEST CHAINS

Each ending is a campaign arc, not a single quest. They can be pursued in
parallel; completing one ends the campaign.

### THE UNIFIER — "One Flag"

**Premise:** Hold a majority of America (26+ states) under one banner for 365
days. The maps stop showing the old borders. You become what the Fracture
unmade.

**Act I — The Claim (Days 1-90)**
- *Quest: "The First Banner"* — Take your first state capital. Any capital.
  The game tracks it. Rulers start taking you seriously. Or start aiming at you.
- *Quest: "The Arithmetic"* — Your advisors present the math: 26 states, 365
  days, and everyone will try to stop you. Choose your first three targets.
  Each choice closes some doors and opens others.
- *Quest: "The Defectors"* — Two minor lords offer to join you. One is sincere.
  One is a spy. Figure out which before you give them troops.

**Act II — The Grind (Days 90-270)**
- *Quest: "The Bread Problem"* — You're winning territory but losing the food
  math. The Union offers a deal: grain for peace. The price is humiliating.
  The alternative is starvation.
- *Quest: "The Betrayal"* — Your most trusted governor stops answering orders.
  They're negotiating with your enemies. Deal with it — diplomatically, or
  otherwise.
- *Quest: "The Winter"* — Your armies are overextended. Winter is coming.
  Choose: pull back and lose momentum, or push through and risk everything.

**Act III — The Crown (Days 270-365)**
- *Quest: "The Last Holdout"* — One section refuses to bend. Their capital is
  a fortress. Their ruler is a legend. Take it, or make a deal that history
  will argue about forever.
- *Quest: "The 365th Day"* — Hold everything for a full year. Survive the
  rebellions, the assassinations, the famines, the betrayals. If you do, the
  maps change. You are the Unifier.

**What it costs:** Everything. Friends, sleep, mercy. The Unifier doesn't get
to be loved. Just obeyed.

### THE KINGMAKER — "The Power Behind"

**Premise:** Never take the top seat. Instead, put your chosen rulers on every
throne and own their debts. Win without a crown.

**Act I — The Debts (Days 1-120)**
- *Quest: "The First Loan"* — A struggling lord needs gold. Lend it. The terms
  are... favorable. To you.
- *Quest: "The Collection"* — Call in the debt. They can't pay. Offer an
  alternative: loyalty. Watch what happens.
- *Quest: "The Network"* — You have three debtors in three sections. They don't
  know about each other. Keep it that way.

**Act II — The Thrones (Days 120-300)**
- *Quest: "The Election"* — A section leader died. Two claimants. Back the
  weaker one — they'll owe you more. Make sure they win.
- *Quest: "The War"* — Your debtors are fighting each other. Both call for
  help. Choose carefully — or play both sides.
- *Quest: "The Ledger"* — Someone found your books. They know everything.
  Silence them, buy them, or... recruit them.

**Act III — The Shadow (Days 300+)**
- *Quest: "The Coronation"* — Your fifth puppet takes a throne. You own five
  sections and hold no titles. The Corridor is impressed. Everyone else is
  terrified.
- *Quest: "The Price"* — One of your kings rebels. They've found their spine.
  Remind them who paid for it — or let them go and watch what happens.

**What it costs:** Trust. No one will ever believe you don't want the crown.
They're right not to.

### THE BREADLORD — "The Hand That Feeds"

**Premise:** Control the food. In a hungry country, whoever feeds the people
rules them — without drawing a weapon.

**Act I — The Grain (Days 1-100)**
- *Quest: "The Elevator"* — Buy a grain elevator in Union territory. It's
  expensive. It's worth it.
- *Quest: "The Drought"* — A bad harvest hits. Prices spike. You have grain.
  Everyone wants it. Choose who eats.
- *Quest: "The Offer"* — The Union offers you a monopoly contract. The terms
  are good. The obligations are... extensive.

**Act II — The Famine (Days 100-250)**
- *Quest: "The Siege"* — A city is starving. You can feed them — for a price.
  The price is loyalty. Watch a ruler sell their soul for bread.
- *Quest: "The Rival"* — Someone else is trying to corner the market. They're
  good. They're ruthless. They're going to lose.
- *Quest: "The Choice"* — Two cities starving. Grain for one. Choose. Live
  with it.

**Act III — The Feast (Days 250+)**
- *Quest: "The Tribute"* — Sections send you tribute — not in gold, in
  obedience. You never raised an army. You never needed to.
- *Quest: "The Last Harvest"* — A blight threatens everything. Save the crop,
  save your power. Fail, and watch it all rot.

**What it costs:** Your soul, one meal at a time. The Breadlord doesn't kill
people. Just lets them choose between obedience and hunger.

### THE GHOST — "Power Without Territory"

**Premise:** Build a mercenary company so feared and so rich that sections pay
you not to march. Win without a country.

**Act I — The Company (Days 1-80)**
- *Quest: "The First Contract"* — Take a job. Any job. Prove you're worth the
  money.
- *Quest: "The Reputation"* — Complete three contracts without breaking one.
  Word spreads. The pay goes up. So do the risks.
- *Quest: "The Betrayal"* — A employer tries to cheat you. Make an example.
  Everyone's watching.

**Act II — The Fear (Days 80-200)**
- *Quest: "The Refusal"* — Turn down a contract from a powerful section. They
  threaten you. Laugh. Then prove why they shouldn't have.
- *Quest: "The Tribute"* — A section pays you NOT to take a contract against
  them. You're now a political force. Without territory. Without titles.
- *Quest: "The Hunt"* — Someone hired assassins to kill you. They're good.
  You're better. Find who sent them.

**Act III — The Legend (Days 200+)**
- *Quest: "The War"* — Two sections go to war. Both want you. Choose — or
  refuse both and watch them destroy each other.
- *Quest: "The Ghost"* — You've become a story mothers tell children. "Be good,
  or the Ghost will come." Decide what that means.

**What it costs:** A home. The Ghost has no country, no family, no rest. Just
the next contract.

### THE SURVIVOR — "One Town"

**Premise:** Keep one town alive through everything — famine, plague, siege,
betrayal — for a full generation (20 years). The smallest ending. The hardest.

**Act I — The Town (Years 1-5)**
- *Quest: "The Choice"* — Pick your town. It should be defensible. It should
  have water. It probably doesn't have both.
- *Quest: "The Wall"* — Build defenses. The town can't afford it. Build them
  anyway.
- *Quest: "The First Winter"* — Survive. Just survive.

**Act II — The Trials (Years 5-15)**
- *Quest: "The Plague"* — Sickness comes. Quarantine or mercy? Choose fast.
- *Quest: "The Siege"* — An army arrives. They want your town. Hold the walls.
- *Quest: "The Betrayal"* — Someone inside opened the gates. Find them. Decide
  what justice means when everyone is starving.

**Act III — The Generation (Years 15-20)**
- *Quest: "The Children"* — The kids born in your town are growing up. They've
  never known peace. Teach them what it's for.
- *Quest: "The Last Year"* — One more year. Hold on. Just one more.

**What it costs:** Everything else. The Survivor doesn't conquer, doesn't rule,
doesn't win. Just endures. And sometimes, that's enough.

## 19. THE RULERS — WHO HOLDS WHAT

The game generates hundreds of rulers, but these are the archetypes — the kinds
of people who rise in each section. Every generated ruler is a variation on
these themes.

### Pacific Compact — The Harbor Lords

**The Port Master:** Controls a major harbor. Rich, connected, paranoid about
blockades. Thinks in terms of tonnage and tariffs. Will sell anything except
the port itself.

**The Tech Baron:** Made money in intelligence or technology. Young, arrogant,
convinced they're smarter than everyone. Often right. Always dangerous.

**The Water Judge:** Settles disputes over rivers and aqueducts. The most
powerful person in the Compact that no one's heard of. Cross them and your
city goes thirsty.

### Mountain Alliance — The Mine Kings

**The Mine Owner:** Sits on gold or metal. Rich, isolated, convinced the
lowlands are coming for what's theirs. Usually right.

**The Pass Keeper:** Controls a mountain pass. Collects tolls. Knows every
trail. Could stop an army with twenty men and the right avalanche.

**The Drifter:** No fixed holdings. Leads a mobile band. Hired by everyone,
trusted by no one. The Alliance's wild card.

### Great Lakes Union — The Grain Lords

**The Quartermaster:** Controls grain elevators and distribution. The most
powerful person in the Union. Never runs for office. Doesn't need to.

**The Factory Boss:** Runs industrial plants. Builds trucks, rifles, parts.
Thinks in terms of output and efficiency. Sees people as... resources.

**The Militia Colonel:** Commands the Union's massive levies. Popular with the
troops. Dangerous to the rulers. Always one bad harvest from a coup.

### Southern Compact — The Warlords

**The Muster Lord:** Can raise an army in days. Charismatic, aggressive,
impatient. Wins battles. Loses peaces.

**The Grudge Keeper:** Old family, old money, old hatreds. Remembers every
slight. Never forgives. Controls territory through fear and tradition.

**The Preacher:** Religious or ideological leader. Commands loyalty that money
can't buy. The South's conscience — or its excuse.

### Lone Star Frontier — The Rangers

**The Ranch King:** Vast lands, countless cattle and horses. Independent,
stubborn, generous to friends and merciless to enemies.

**The Oil Baron:** Controls refineries or wells. Rich beyond measure. Knows
that energy is power and acts like it.

**The Ranger Captain:** Leads the Frontier's legendary mounted troops. Lives
in the saddle. Dies in the saddle. Everyone respects them. No one controls them.

### Atlantic Corridor — The Ledger Lords

**The Banker:** Controls money flows. Never raises a weapon. Doesn't need to.
Can bankrupt a section with a signature.

**The Spymaster:** Runs intelligence networks. Knows everyone's secrets. Sells
them to the highest bidder — which is usually the Corridor itself.

**The Governor:** Old political family. Knows where the bodies are buried
because they buried some. Charming. Ruthless. Essential.

## 20. FAMILY TREES — HOW POWER INHERITS

Power in the divided states is family business. Here's how it works in each
section:

### The Compact: Corporate Dynasties

Pacific families operate like companies — mergers through marriage, hostile
takeovers through... other means. The harbor lords intermarry to consolidate
port control. Children are educated in finance and logistics. Disputes are
settled in boardrooms, or not at all.

**Inheritance:** Eldest child inherits, but competence matters more than birth
order. An incompetent heir will be "retired" by the family council.

### The Alliance: Clan Holdings

Mountain families are clans — extended networks bound by blood and mutual
defense. Mine ownership passes through the male line (tradition), but pass
control often goes to the most capable (necessity). Feuds last generations.

**Inheritance:** Strict primogeniture for mines, merit-based for military
commands. The two often conflict.

### The Union: Political Machines

Union families are political — they control elevators, factories, and votes.
Marriages are alliances. Children are groomed for leadership from birth.
The quartermasters are the real power; the elected officials are the face.

**Inheritance:** The family business goes to whoever can hold it. Often the
most ruthless, not the eldest.

### The South: Honor and Blood

Southern families take honor seriously — insults are remembered, debts are
paid, and blood is blood. Feuds are sacred. Alliances are sealed in marriage
and broken in war.

**Inheritance:** Eldest son inherits. Daughters marry to secure alliances.
Bastards... complicate things.

### The Frontier: Merit and Grit

Frontier families respect competence over birth. A ranch hand who proves
themselves can marry into the family. An incompetent heir will lose the ranch.

**Inheritance:** The capable inherit. The incapable get a horse and best wishes.

### The Corridor: Old Money

Corridor families are old — some trace back to before the Fracture. They
operate through trusts, foundations, and quiet ownership. Power is inherited,
but it's also... managed.

**Inheritance:** Complex trusts and legal structures. The family lawyer is
more powerful than most governors.
